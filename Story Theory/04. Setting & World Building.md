# Story Construction

## Part 3: Setting

I.  INTRO: 
1. 
The standard def of setting is “the time and place in which a story is 
set.”  But setting is more than that.  It is an added dimension of your story.  
And like plot or character, it should develop. 
 
For ex:  Lord of the Flies by William Golding.  The island changes 
from a paradise to a trash heap. This reflects the deterioration of the 
character’s values on the island. It reflects the breakdown of the 
community.  
 
For ex: Working Girl (1988 film).  The hero’s world changes from 
cubicle to office.  This is an encapsulation of the entire story.  Those two 
worlds represent the hero’s change. 
2. 
How important is setting to a story?  Of the 5 possible kinds of 
conflict — 1) internal, 2) interpersonal, 3) hero against setting, 4) hero 
against society, 5) hero against nature — three of them pertain to setting.  
Setting is an important dimension, often overlooked and under 
developed.

Good development of setting gives your story more variety of 
conflict. 
 
Sophisticated development of setting distinguishes great writing 
from mediocre writing. 
 
Setting is where you have lots of opportunity to use comparison & 
contrast, as well as metaphor.  
II.  WRITING VISUALLY 
1. 
One way to develop setting is by using visual imagery. 80 percent of 
human perceptual is visual. Other senses are important, too, but visual is 
dominant. Visual imagery makes your writing come alive. Key’s to doing it 
well are: 
 
a) Be specific (not “trees,” they’re “ancient gnarled oaks.”) Don’t say 
“the fire burned.”  Say, “he was attacked by a red and yellow storm of 
flame.” 
 
b) Use combos.  (Don’t write “The city buildings were tall. It was a 
red sunset. There were deep shadows.”  Instead write, “the red sun sank 
behind tall buildings, casting deep shadows across the city street.”

c) Don’t describe too much. Use visual imagery sparingly. One 
phrase or sentence, here and there.  Long passages about the wind in the 
trees is boring.

2. 
Another way to create visual drama is with setting dynamics. The 
worlds, arenas, & societies in which you set your story should not be 
static, but DYNAMIC.  
 
Just like you design an opponent to define a facet of the hero, you 
use also the setting to show who the hero is, either by contrast or by 
compliment. The setting can either represent the hero’s values or 
oppose the hero’s values.   
 
For ex, Music Man (broadway musical and film).  The hero is a slick 
con man. The town was extremely naive to make Harold Hill look more 
powerful in comparison.  The setting showed who the hero was by 
contrast.  When the town began to wise up to who Harold Hill was, the 
hero wised up to who he, himself, was. 
 
For ex, Lawrence of Arabia (1962 film).  The desert tribes were 
unintegrated and  weak.  Just like Lawrence.  The setting showed who the 
hero was — metaphorically — by compliment.  As Lawrence put the 
tribes together to form one nation, he was also putting himself together 
form a whole man. The development of the setting tracked the 
development of Lawrence.

For ex, Dr. Zhivago (1965 film).  The hero was a poet living in pre-
communist Russia and was considered to be a national treasure.  The 
setting complimented the hero.  After the 1918 revolution took over, the 
setting contrasted who the hero was — a poet in a communist state.  (The 
ice world was a metaphor for the coldness of the new society; the yellow 
flowers were a metaphor for the world of love into which the hero 
escaped.) 
 
For ex, Batman.  The toxic chemical plant compliments who the 
villain is. 
 
These are setting dynamics — your various aspects of setting 
develop, and can compliment or contrast who the hero is.   
III.
THE 3 ASPECTS TO SETTING: 
 
1.   
The World. 
 
 
This is the big overall setting of your story.  You set your story 
in the city, the house, the forest, etc.  You story can have more than one 
world (Dances With Wolves & Wizard of Oz) but using too many worlds 
weakens your drama.  Usually one, two, maybe three.  Four worlds, and 
you story loses energy.  (This is a weakness of the heroic journey form.) 
© Chas Holloway 2022
Pt 3: Setting

2. 
The Arena. 
 
 
This is the specific place within your world where the current 
drama is taking place.  Named after the arena of the gladiators where hero 
and opponents fight it out.   
 
If your story world is a house, areas could be the dining room, 
bedroom, living room, back yard, etc (Who’s Afraid of Virginia Wolf). If your 
story world is the city of New York, your arenas could be the work 
cubicles, the boss’ office, the reastaurant, back at home, etc. Working Girl 
— Manhattan & Queens, the ferry was a passage between worlds.  The 
arena is your immediate, current place where the drama is happening. 
 
3. 
The Wall of the Arena. 
 
 
The more defined the wall of your arena is, the more conflict 
and suspense you can create.  For ex, there’s a criminal in the kitchen. The 
hero looks for a gun in his living room.  The wall between the two rooms is 
the wall between life and death.  The more defined the wall, the more 
powerful the scene.  Audience thinks, “Don’t cross that line!” (Suddenly 
with Frank Sinatra.) 
 
For ex, Star Wars.  The kill the death star sequence was more 
exciting because the ships had to fly through the narrow channel — not 
shoot at it from a distance. 
 
For ex, Agatha Christie’s Ten Little Indians.  Ten characters in a 
house in the mountains locked in by an ice storm — characters get

knocked off, one by one — and there’s no escape.  Because she defined 
the wall of the arena: an impassable ice world.  
 
4. 
The Community (or Society). 
 
 
This is different from world or arena.  Community is a gathering 
of people (usually in a specific location) and the values they share.  For ex, 
in Dances With Wolves, set during the Civil War.  The American 
communities are in chaos — the shared values of the people are insanity 
and war.  The hero leaves and goes to the Indian village where the shared 
values of the people is personal integrity and living in harmony with nature.  
The story had two opposed communities (or societies).   
 
WORLD refers to the physical properties of a general place.    
 
 
COMMUNITY refers to the values of the people who share or fight 
for it.  
 
ARENA is where the current (moment by moment)  conflict is taking 
place. 
 
More about constructing communities (societies) later.

V.
SETTING AS METAPHOR 
 
Another way to create visual drama is to use your worlds (and  
arenas) as metaphors.   
 
For ex, Paris, Texas (film).  We meet the hero as he walks out of the 
desert.  The desert is a severe, barren place.  It’s a metaphor for the 
psychological place the hero was living in.  It’s a metaphor for his internal 
state.  
 
For ex, Say Anything (1989 film).  The hero loses the girl, so he 
walks alone in the Seattle rain.  The lonely streets and rain are a metaphor 
for his lonliness and suffering. 
© Chas Holloway 2022
Pt 3: Setting

Some setting metaphors are cliches.  Used thousands of times.  But 
they’re still not obvious. Not like dialogue cliches.  
 
Important: 
 
Metaphors are not marathon runners.  Don’t write: “The ship of 
state navigates through the seas of confusion only to encounter the 
squalls of riots and the storm of war — while an ocean of desperation is all 
around.” 
 
Instead write, “The storm of war was rising.”    
 
Metaphors are sprinters. 
VI.
THREE SETTING DEVICES 
 
1.  
Utopia and Dystopia. 
 
 
Utopia is when the Land (resources), people’s values and 
technology are in harmony.  The structure of the society dampens 
conflicts.  People are helped by the way the community works.   
 
 
Dystopia is when the land (resources), people’s values and 
technology are in disharmony. The structure of the society creates 
conflicts. People are injured by the way the community works. 
© Chas Holloway 2022
Pt 3: Setting

For ex, The Time Machine by H.G. Wells.  The utopia of the future 
was undermined by the dystopian Morlocks. Two worlds — utopia above 
ground and dystopia under ground.   
 
For ex, Defending Your Life (1991 film).  The hero dies and goes to 
heaven (a utopia).  But he can’t relate to existing without fear, so he’s in 
conflict with utopia.   
 
Utopia and dystopia can be used in an arena to create a temporary 
effect. The perfect day, or moment, or evening.  
 
For ex, The Wild Bunch (1969 film). The hero and his allies 
experience a perfect day in utopia before the day of the big battle 
sequence. This makes the battle scene more brutal in contrast. 
 
Lost (TV show). The airline passengers share a perfect evening 
under the stars around the fire. This makes the statement that, at heart, in 
spite of their conflicts, they are a community.  
2.
Microcosm and Macrocosm.   
 
Using a MICROCOSM means showing a miniature world within your 
story.

For ex, Citizen Kane (1941 film).  The snow globe in the dying man’s 
hand represented the most cherished part of the hero.  It was also a 
metaphor for the central conflict of the story, “What is Rosebud?” 
 
For ex, The Wizard of Oz (1939 film).  The wicked witch could see 
Dorothy and her allies in her crystal ball.  This showed the witch’s power

seeing is really just part of a larger world. 
 
For ex: Working Girl (1988 film).  The world the hero lives in changes 
from cubicle to office. In the last scene of the film, camera pulls back to 
show that the hero’s little office is just one of thousands in the city.

VII.
NATURAL SETTINGS 
 
Natural settings are places in nature. 
 
Never select a natural setting for your story without a reason.  Every 
natural setting carries with it built-in meanings.  You need to know the 
built-in meanings of places like hills, islands, rivers, etc. 
1. Ocean. 
 
Has three aspects.  First, the ultimate two-dimensional landscape, a 
flat table as far as the eye can see.  This makes the surface of the ocean 
an abstract world.  A place where a contest is the only thing happening.  
Almost like reader’s theater, or a play against a hard cyc.   
 
Second, the ocean deep is a three-dimensional world where all 
creatures are weightless, and creatures live at every level.   
 
Third, it can turn against you. The surface can be peaceful, 
stagnant, or violent. 
 
Positive:  Ocean deep is a natural utopia.  Often a dreamworld.  Is 
ancient.  Sunken treasures.  
 
Negative:  The surface is a place of depravation. The ocean deep is 
a place of terror.  A graveyard, an ancient world, prehistoric creatures,

IX.
MAN MADE SETTINGS 
 
Man made settings are more valuable to you as a writer than natural 
spaces.  They solve one of the most difficult problems you have: how to 
express a society.   
 
Every man-made space can be an expression, in microcosm, of the 
hero and the society in which he lives.   
 
Here are some of the major man made spaces that can help you 
express society: 
The House: 
 
This is the primary statement of who the hero is.  Things in the house 
represent the growth of a person’s mind and the state of their mind in the 
present.    
 
It’s the central unit of social life and the central arena of drama.  
Writers must strongly consider what role the house plays in their story.   
 
The house is unsurpassed as a place of intimacy.   
 
You can fill it with visual oppositions to express the house to its 
fullest dramatic potential. (think: The Addams Family) 
 
The house can be the cradle, shell or nest of the hero — a place of 
safety.  Or, through the windows, the eyes of the house, it’s a place where 
you’re called to adventure.  (Slow Glass) 
 
The house is rooted in the ground.  It’s a place where the hero is 
attached to a deep foundation.

The Road. 
 
The road is the opposite of the house.  The house calls us to nestle 
in.  The road is the call to go out, to explore, to become something new.   
 
The house is vertical writing — the road is horizontal writing. 
 
The road is always tenuous.  A single, thin line, surrounded by 
wilderness.   
 
The road requires courage.  But it promises a destination worth 
reaching. 
 
Myth stories are centered on this fundamental difference between 
house and road (Lord of the Rings.  The Odyssey).

can’t build rising conflict is the hero encounters a number of separate 
opponents on the road.  The solution for this is the vehicle. 
 
Rule of thumb: the bigger the vehicle, the easier to bring opponents 
along for the ride.  These are ongoing opponents.

The City. 
 
This is the biggest man made structure.  It has thousands of

XI.
TIME 
 
Just like technology is a type of setting, so is time.  It can be a 
major part of the construction of your world.   
 
Here’s a collection of useful ways to express time in your story: 
1. Time as a Setting: Use of Past or Future.  
 
When you set a story in the past, you’re not writing a story about the 
past.  When you set a story in the future, you’re not writing about the 
future. Your writing a story about how things are today.  
 
Even a gothic romance novel — setting the story in an ancestral 
castle intensifies the love story.  
 
The Star Trek stories intensified commentaries on the issues of the 
1960s. (Stratus, The black/white guy. Day of the Dove.)  
 
2. How to Use Time to Develop the World: 
 
Time in stories is about showing how the world develops — and how 
that makes the characters and storyline develop. 
© Chas Holloway 2022
Pt 3: Setting

Seasons. 
 
You can set your story, or a part of your story, within a particular 
season.  Each season conveys certain meanings about the hero or the 
world. (end of Love Story, hero was in winter) 
 
If you show the change of seasons, you give the audience a 
powerful expression about growth or decay of the hero, or the world. 
 
If you cover all four seasons, you create a circular story that says in 
some way things remain the same — return to the status quo.  A whole 
cycle of seasons emphasizes how characters or the world is connected to 
the land. 
Rituals and Holidays. 
 
A ritual is a belief system that has been translated into a set of 
actions that are repeated at intervals.  A ritual in your story makes a 
strong visual statement.  
 
For ex, church or temple.  A Moose Lodge meeting.  A harvest 
celebration. 
 
A holiday expands the scope of the ritual to a national (world) level, 
giving it large social impact. 
 
If you use a ritual or holiday in your story, examine the philosophy of 
it — you can use it to make a comment.   
© Chas Holloway 2022
Pt 3: Setting

The Single Day. 
 
This is an increment of time that gives you a specific effect.  Instead 
of showing a single character over a long development, you show a 
number of characters all acting at the same time, right now, today.  This 
gives your story a sense of compression. 
 
You can use a 12-hour clock.   One day, or one night.  The 
audience senses everything will be resolves at the end of 12 hours, and 
you get a built-in sense of urgency. 
Ferris Bueller’s Day Off - one day 
American Graffiti - one night 
The Party 
 
 
 
You can use a 24-hour clock.  This is different.  You lessen the 
urgency and increase the sense of the circular.  No matter what happens, 
you return to the beginning.  A new day.  To start all over again.   
Ulysses. 
Ground Hog Day. 
 
The 24-hour day has the same kind of effect as the 4 seasons.  They 
both tend to emphasize society rather than the individual.  The second 
major effect of this technique — it emphasizes the “everyday” quality of 
the story.  A day in the life.

XII.
FOUR STAGES OF CIVILIZATION 
1. Nomadic World. 
 
Hunter gatherer world.  You travel from place to place, set up an 
encampment for a period of time, then move on.  You spend your life on 
the move, following the migrating animals. 
 
This is a might-makes-right world.  There is no such thing as 
morality.  If you’re strong, you win.  If you’re weak, you lose.   
 
Emphasizes fighting skills.  Physical action.  Early humans had to 
survive by fighting and killing.  
 
There’s no commitment to a society.  It’s every man for himself. 
 
Humans are just one of the animals, and this is the kind of life people 
had when stories first appeared.  
 
That’s why early heroic journey stories are concerned with the 
question, “What does it mean to be human?”

2. The Village. 
 
A fragile place of civilization surrounded by hostile wilderness.  
People band together and settle in one place to have a higher quality of 
life.

This is the beginning of commerce.  The marketplace.  The concept 
of property and wealth are created.  
 
A higher concept of morality develops.  The right way to live is now 
to help the community.  Not only self profit, but also profit for the society. 
 
The best way to live is to profit yourself, but to also help the 
community profit and get stronger.  
 
There’s a system of laws.  It’s no longer good to kill indiscriminately.  
The individual now has to consider the well-being of others as well as 
himself.  
 
A lonely outpost. (Dances With Wolves.) 
 
A village in the jungle.  (African Queen.) 
 
A space station. (Silent Running) 
 
The Englishman Who Went Up a Hill and Came Down a Mountain. 
3. The City World. 
 
The place of the average hero; the everyman. 
 
The is the village grown up to be big.  The village is personal — 
everybody has a strong a personality.  But in the city, the individual is 
almost invisible. 
 
Ironically, when you’re surrounded by the most people, you are also 
the most alone.

There’s a new kind of morality in the city — in the village it was clear 
which laws were good for the community, and which were bad.  But in the 
city it’s no longer obvious. 
 
The city is a world of interpretations,  not black and white.  No such 
things as good an evil.  Right and wrong are a matter of judgement — and 
always confusing. 
 
The hero in the city world stories is interested in finding justice. 
 
(Detective & police stories. Modern dramas. Courtroom dramas.) 
 
4. The Tyranny World. 
 
The city has grown into a monstrous machine.  Society is completely 
oppressive.  There’s no freedom anywhere. The concept of the individual 
totally disappears.  
 
No place for a free thinker in the machine.  All people are supposed 
to be subservient to the Great Order. To Dear Leader. 
 
The only way to be moral in a tyranny is to work against the society.  
Try to bring the tyranny down. 
 
The only way to escape the tyranny is to retreat into your own mind.

Conclusion: The Cycle: 
 
 
 
These 4 stages of society are a cycle.  The tyranny world can’t be 
sustained because there is no creative force left — so it collapses.  Then, 
civilization goes back to the first stage — the nomadic world — as a few 
miserable survivors pick through the rubble of the civilization that used to 
be and try to survive.  
 
And the cycle starts all over again.  
How do you break this cycle? 
 
We’re living in the phase between city world and tyranny world now. 
Quickly shifting towards tyranny. Which means society is about to collapse 
— and we’ll soon be running around with stones and clubs again 
(according to this cycle). 
 
That’s why youth is so depressed and hedonistic. They only see 
destruction in their future.
