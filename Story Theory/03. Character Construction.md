CHARACTER
Part One
INTRO:
1.
In the last session, we talked about PLOT. 
The four D’s (engine)
How to sequences your story. 
Something goes wrong (bending, little c between big)
But plot isn’t the whole story (pun).
Equally important is CHARACTER DEVELOPMENT
Audiences empathize (feel with) with the CHARACTERS in 
your story.  They want to follow your characters PERSONALITY 
and experience how those characters cope with the CONFLICTS 
they encounter.
2.
Old writer’s adage: there are THREE WAYS of showing 
character in a story:
By what the character SAYS
By what the character DOES
By what OTHER characters say about him
This tells you how to do exposition. 
But it doesn’t tell you how to create interesting characters. 

3.
Another adage: there’s a difference between “ROUND” and 
“FLAT” characters.
Fallacy::
“All your characters should be round, not flat.” This is 
supposed to mean they should be “developed.” They should have 
“Three Dimensions.” Not just “One Dimension.”  
This comes from a 1927 book, Aspects of the Novel, by E. 
M. Forester.
He coined the terms “flat” and “round” characters.
He defined a “flat” character as “the embodiment of a single 
idea.”  
The truth is, “flat” characters can be the most interesting 
ones in your story.
Alice in Wonderland.
Comic characters: 1 to 3 character traits.
Example: Great Expectations. Miss Havisham has been 
sitting in the same chair in the same old bedroom for 30 years, 
wearing her wedding dress that’s faded from white to yellow.  
Cobwebs all over the room.  
She’s the “embodiement of a single idea.”
In one description, we see what happened to her in the past 
and how it destroyed her. She was the most memorable character 
in the book.  
E. M. Forester called “round” characters an “organization of 
different traits.”  
Still vague.  Just what does this mean?

I’m going to explain, but first, let’s talk about CONFLICT. 
CONFLICT
Conflict in your story should occur almost constantly.  
Every scene should have one or more conflicts. 
Occasionally, you might write a scene that has NO conflict – 
but if so, it’s rare, there’s a specific reason for it. 
Question:
How do you keep constant conflict from being tedious?  How 
do you keep one conflict after another from wearing the audience 
out?
Answer:
Use different KINDS of conflicts.  Use variety.  You shift from 
one type of conflict to another.
5 KINDS OF CONFLICT:
There are FIVE possible kinds of conflict.  They are:
1. Internal conflict.  
    
This is the psychological war going on inside your character’s 
head.  This is someone wrestling with his or her own inconsistent 
values.  

This is ROCKY telling himself, “Sure I’ll do the right thing.  I’ll 
fight the fight. But who am I to go against the world champion?  
He’s the best – I’m gonna get creamed.” 
This is LAWRENCE OF ARABIA telling himself: “To lead 
these tribes, I have to be a tyrant — but I have to stop because I 
enjoy it too much.”
In a NOVEL, the storyteller can explain a characters internal 
conflicts.
You can use stream of consciousness.
In a SCREENPLAY, you have to create propinquity.  
“PROPINQUITY” means “proximity and kinship.”  You have 
to create a character that your hero can talk to, so we know what 
the Hero is thinking.
2. Interpersonal Conflict.
This is the conflict between the various characters in your 
story. 
For example: 
a) Johnny, tells his family at the dinner table, “I’m leaving 
home and going to college.”
b) Father says, “No! You’re going to stay right here and work 
at my plumbing company like I always planned for you.”  

c) Mother says, “Leave him alone!  Let him go to college!  
Oh, but, Johnny, what are you going to do for money?  Here, take 
this from the cookie jar.  A thousand dollars.”  
d) His Sister says, “John, what are you doing?  You can’t 
leave me alone with these two – they’ll tear me apart.  Don’t go -- 
you have to stay for my sake.”  
(now you have 3 scenes)
The key to writing interpersonal conflict is to develop a 
unique VALUE SYSTEM for each of your characters. 
Interpersonal conflict is all about your character’s value systems 
clashing.  
George Clayton Johnson said, “Dialogue is what 
characters do to each other.”  
Example: the film, MR. ROGERS NEIGHBORHOOD.
3. Hero Against a Setting.
This is the conflict between the hero and his IMMEDIATE 
surroundings. The hero is in an unfriendly place – and the place 
seems to be at war with him.
For example, 
The hero goes to the DMV. It’s severe and threatening. The 
receptionist is cold. The chairs are full and there’s no place to sit. 
The sign says “Now serving H-974.” The only place to stand is 
where somebody spilled their coke.  

For example, 
Indiana Jones is exploring a cave that has hidden traps all 
around – and the skeletons of people who tried to walk through it 
before him.
For example, 
Film (Best Picture, 1979): Kramer Vs. Kramer
The Hero’s wife left him suddenly. Now, he has to fix 
breakfast for his 5-year-old. He tries to make French toast, but 
he’s never used the kitchen before. He gets burned on the stove. 
He can’t find the bowls. He tries to mix eggs in a juice glass and 
stuff bread into it. 
He’s at war in his marriage, at war with his kid, and he’s at 
war with the kitchen.  
4.  Hero Against Society.
This is a more sophisticated kind of conflict. It’s one of the 
keys to great writing.  
In your story, you have your central communities (home, 
school, workplace, etc.) but you also have the wider community 
— society at large.
For example, 
It’s a Wonderful Life. George Bailey, a small banker and 
family man, wants to be an architect in the big city. He doesn’t 

want to be in the hick town of Bedford Falls. He’s in conflict with 
the entire town.  
To make this work, 
First, you have to establish what the hero’s values are – he 
wants to be a big shot architect.
Second, you have to create a world that represents the 
opposite (a small American town where nobody is a superstar). 
Third, you need to create a reason why the hero is stuck 
there (he’s trapped by his family and business responsibilities). 
Now you have an “always.” Anywhere in your story, you can 
show the hero being repressed by his hick town. 
More Examples:
Dr. Zhivago – the hero is a poet — in conflict with the 
communist dictatorship that says there should be no such thing as 
“individuality.”
Gandhi – the hero is in conflict with the British bureaucracy 
that controls India.
Had two wider societies: the Indian people and the Brit 
bureaucracy. 
Masterful stories develop Hero Vs. Society conflict to a high 
degree. (More in session 3: setting)

5. Hero Against Nature.   
Hero vs. Society can be personified. You can characters that 
represent some aspect of the  society that’s repressing the hero. 
But w Hero vs. Nature, the hero is in conflict with something 
that’s NOT personal, at all. Nature just IS – and the hero has to 
find a way to cope with it. 
Examples:
The film (1991), The Perfect Storm 
The novel, The Old Man and the Sea
The film (1995), Apollo 13
The Odyssey begins with: “Tell me, Oh Muse, of Odysseus 
who was driven on far journeys by the Gods…”
Odysseus is in conflict with the Gods, which are the forces 
behind the forces of nature. Poseidon makes the violent storm 
blow. (obsolete idea; hard to sell)

CHARACTER
Part 2
DIMENSIONS of the Hero
E.M. Forester says characters should be 3-dimensional.”
There are actually FIVE dimensions to your hero. They are:
CHANGE – this is your hero’s moral range. All stories have 
morals. (In stories, “moral” means how a character acts towards 
other characters — as opposed to “psychology.”) 
DESIRE – this is what gives your hero (and your story) 
continuity. This is where you put the signposts that orient the 
audience. The desire line.
MOTIVE – this is WHY your hero acts. This is what the 
audience emotionally relates to, the source of the audience bond.  
This is how you get empathy for your hero. This is your hero’s 
value system.
DEPTH (PSYCHOLOGICAL / EMOTIONAL) – this is what 
causes the hero to change his values or to deepen his values. 
GHOST – this is what gives the hero the values he comes 
into the story with.
These are the five possible aspects of a character. There are 
NO OTHER POSSIBILITIES. Let’s talk about each one of these 
dimensions.

HOW TO DEVELOP THE 5 DIMENSIONS:
INTRO:
In last session on PLOT, we talked about how to sequence 
your story
How to use the four D’s. 
How to use the principle of SOMETHING GOES WRONG.
Your sequences, 4 D’s, and something goes wrongs — 
should be mostly about developing characters. So, here’s how 
you develop characters.
(I’m focusing here on how to develop your HERO’s 
dimensions. But the same principles apply to other characters, as 
well.)
I. The Hero’s Change:
Definition of CHANGE:
The Hero’s RANGE of CHANGE is set by his Moral Need at 
the beginning of the story, and his Self Revelation at the end.
The hero has one set of values at the beginning – another 
set of values at the end. These are the two OPPOSITE POLES of 
the hero’s change.

For Example,
A Christmas Carol.  Scrooge is a selfish miser at the 
beginning. He becomes generous and caring at the end. His 
range of change is “selfish to participant in the community.”
Rocky. At the beginning, he’s an under confident guy who 
lets the world tell him who he is. At the end, he’s a self-
empowered world champion. His range of change is, 
“underconfident punching bag to world champion.”
The hero’s change can be small or big.
A SMALL change tends to be more believable but less 
interesting.
A LARGE  change tends to be less believable but more 
interesting.
For Example,
The Wizard of Oz.  Dorothy’s change is small. She goes 
from dreaming of life over the rainbow to realizing she can find 
happiness in her own back yard.  
Great movie, but her range of change was NOT where the 
interest was. 
Rocky. Hero’s change is big. He’s an unknown club fighter 
who becomes a world champion. Not believable, but it says, 
anything is possible in America. (Including a nobody winning Best 
Picture.)

Some traditional character changes:
From Beast to Human (Frankenstein, King Kong)
From Child to Adult (Mr. Smith Goes to Washington, Big)
From Adult to Leader (Gandhi, Lawrence of Arabia)
From Cynic to Participant (Casablanca, Witness)
From Leader to Tyrant (Red River, All the Kings Men)
From Leader to Visionary ( Moses, Gandhi)
From Adult to Child (The Heartbreak Kid, The Incredible 
Shrinking Man)
This is not an exhaustive list. But these changes are always 
interesting. 
How to Develop the Hero’s CHANGE:
1.  At the beginning of the story, take time to show who the 
hero is and what he believes. Let him or her talk. Let the audience 
get to know them. 
2.  When the hero picks a desire, there are TWO 
STRATEGIES you can choose between to develop his change.
a) Hero picks MORAL course of action (Rocky)

b) Hero picks an IMMORAL course of action (1987 film: 
Can’t Buy Me Love)
3.  
If the Hero picks a MORAL course of action (Rocky) 
you will emphasize his INTERNAL conflicts.  (“Am I good 
enough?” “Why am I putting myself through this?”)
     
If the hero picks an IMMORAL course (Can’t Buy Me 
Love), you will emphasize his INTERPERSONAL conflicts (the 
other characters react to him becoming more and more immoral.)
(More examples: All the Kings Men, Red River, Dr 
Strangelove, Harold & Maude)
4.  At the end of the story, when the hero undergoes a self 
revelation, he realizes the invalidity of his old values. 
a) if he chose a moral course of action (Rocky), he 
realizes his internal conflicts were invalid.
b) if he chose an immoral course of action (Can’t Buy Me 
Love), he realizes his actions have been invalid. 
In both cases, he sees how his beliefs have caused him to 
have a low quality life.  
5. New Equillibrium at end of story: the hero either 
a) takes on new values, or 
b) he can’t take on new values, or 

c)
he realizes he should have taken on new values, but 
it’s too late. 
He’s either transformed into a better person, or he’s 
destroyed.  
He either rises to a new height. (Rocky)
Or falls to a new low. (Oedipus Rex)
II. The Hero’s Desire:
Definition of Desire:
Desire is the main thing your hero wants. It’s the thing he’s 
trying to achieve. 
In a love story, it’s get the girl or the boy.  
In a war story, it’s capture the territory.  
In a detective story it’s catch the criminal.
We’ve talked about the DESIRE LINE already. How the 
Hero’s desires can move from LOW  to HIGH. That means…
LOW desires are SELFISH desires.  Or ANIMAL desires.  
Or CHILD desires.  Or MACHINE desires.
SELFISH desires: the hero looks out for himself. He doesn’t 
care about anyone else. Other characters don’t matter. The 
community doesn’t matter.

ANIMAL desires: things like food, territory, sex, fighting. You 
can use this dramatically (Rocky: “You fight like a gorilla”). You 
can use this comically (Jerry Lewis.)
CHILD desires: things like seeking approval, attention, 
getting revenge (different from seeking justice), living in a fantasy 
world.  
MACHINE desires: being part of a bureaucracy, being an 
unquestioning soldier or gov’t operative.
Examples:
It’s a Mad Mad World.  All the characters fight to get the 
treasure, all selfish reasons.
Terminator — Machine is chasing the Hero. 
The Nutty Professor — a nerdy professor becomes Barry 
Love to satisfy his animal desires.
Big also Pee Wee’s Big Adventure. Child desire. 
HIGH desires include other people. The hero is not just out 
for himself, but for the community. The hero is concerned about a 
family, a town, a community, a team, etc.  
Examples:
Dances With Wolves. The hero wants to protect the Indian 
community from being destroyed by the settlers.  

The Hero’s desire doesn’t have to move from low to high 
(like in the examples I gave previously).  They can move from 
high to low.
For Example,
All the Kings Men (1949 film): at beginning of story, Willie 
Stark wants to help the community. By the end end, he just lusts 
for power.
The Ruling Class (1972 film) — Hero goes from wanting to 
be Jesus to being Jack the Ripper.
How to Develop the Hero’s Desire:
We’ve covered a lot of this when we talked about building 
and bending the Hero’s desire line in Plot. 
QUICK OVERVIEW (this has been covered):
1.
At the beginning of your story, you usually don’t write desire.  
You spend time developing other stuff — who the hero is and why 
he or she has a low quality life. 
Some stories, however, have a running start – they open 
with immediate desire. We’ll talk about that later when we go into 
genres and conventions.

2.
We talked about how to BUILD the hero’s desire line.
We talked about how to BEND the hero’s desire line using 
“Some thing Goes Wrong.”
We talked about the ENGINE of the story (the Four D’s) and 
about SEQUENCING.
3.
We can also add: the Hero can questions his desire.  “Do I 
want this for me? Or do I want it for some higher purpose?” “What 
am I really after?” 
4. 
At end of the story, the hero either ATTAINS his desire.  Or 
does NOT attain his desire.
Or TRANSCENDS wanting his desire. 
III. The Hero’s Motivation:
Definition of Motivation:
Motive is the REASON the hero acts. It’s his or her reason 
behind wanting something. (“In Spanish, the word for “why” is “for 
what?”: por que?”)
People’s desires are always organized into hierarchies. 
When you do things, you have reasons within reasons, like 
wheels within wheels. 

For example,
The hero finds a winning lottery ticket. His main goal is the 
treasure — but he has REASONS for wanting the money. He 
journeys across the country to cash it in because…
He wants to fit in at the country club and score with chicks.
He wants to get out of his dead end job and buy a house in 
the countryside
He wants to pay for his blind daughter’s eye operation.
More examples:
A wife throws her boyfriend out of the house and changes 
the locks – because her last boyfriend emptied her bank account 
and she’s paranoid it will happen again. 
Dog Day Afternoon (1975 film, Best Screenplay). The hero 
robs a bank and holds everyone hostage because he there’s no 
other way he can help his family. The audience understands why 
he needs to succeed, so they empathize with him even though 
they know taking hostages is wrong.
By giving your hero the right motivation, you can get the 
audience to ROOT for him, even if he’s doing something 
obviously immoral.
Motives are EMOTIONAL. We naturally empathize with 
people’s reasons. Motives are what cause the audience to identify 
with your hero. A writer can get the audience to root for ANY kind 
of hero if he uses the right motivations.

For Example,
Thelma and Louise (1991 film, Best Screenplay).  They 
drive across the country, getting into trouble BECAUSE they have 
to escape the slavery of their bad marriages. In the end, because 
the SYSTEM wants to enslave them, and to be free at all costs, 
they drive off a cliff.  
When you write clear motivations, the audience emotionally 
bonds with your characters, and understands them.  
NEVER give your hero an action unless you understand 
WHY the hero acts. The writer has to understand the hero’s 
motivations better than the hero does.
 
How to Develop the Hero’s Motive:
1.
The Inciting Incident causes the hero’s first desire.  
The thing that happens to the hero has to be a sufficient 
motivation to cause him to pick a course of action. If the hero’s 
reason for this first desire is weak, the entire rest of the story will 
seem weak.    
This is where you first finesse the audience into rooting for 
your hero.
2.
The hero has SHALLOW reasons for acting.

At first, your hero’s reasons for acting are not deep. He or 
she draws from comfortable opinions. The hero thinks in clichés.  
Not deep reasoning. 
He tinks: “Nothing ventured, nothing gained.” Or “I’d better 
take advantage of this opportunity.” Or “Somebody’s got to do 
this.”
3.
The Hero has to put EFFORT into attaining his desire.
The hero soon realizes that this desire is a huge 
undertaking. He didn’t know it was going to be so much work. 
Therefore, cliché reasons for acting won’t do anymore. He has to 
look deeper within himself and confront why he really wants this 
thing.
The hero questions his own values. 
4.
The hero is reduced to PRIMAL drives.
As your story approaches the battle sequence, the hero’s 
reasons for wanting his desire go beyond intellect. He has 
stopped thinking about his motivations. He operates out of raw, 
primal drives. 
Raw courage. Passion. 
Some genres – like war stories, sports stories, thrillers – are 
built to emphasize this stage.

The deepest reasons for wanting something is not 
intellectual, but come from primal drives and urges. 
5.
The Hero’s SELF-REVELATION.
At the end of your story, there are FOUR possible things that 
can happen:
a) The hero ATTAINS his desire – therefore, his motivations 
were all VALID, and he’s been vindicated.
b) The hero DOES NOT attain his desire – therefore, his 
motivations could be either invalid or valid, but he or she will 
never know which. 
c) The hero TRANSCENDS wanting his desire – therefore, 
his motivations were all INVALID.
d) Sometimes the hero attains his desire but winds up 
CONFUSED because he has attained it. 
For Example,
The Graduate (1968 film, nominated Best Picture).  (Now 
what?”)
The Candidate (1974 film, Best Screenplay). (“Now what?”)
(This is a device to give your story ending some irony.)

IV. The Hero’s Depth:
Definition of Depth:
Depth is NOT just your hero’s complexity or profundity of 
thought.  It also refers to the stages of your hero’s intensity of 
emotion. And the intensity of revealing your hero’s psychological 
makeup. 
There are three levels to your hero’s depth.  
1.  CHARACTERISTICS. These are surface things. For ex, 
the hero has a PhD. He plays the violin. He drives a Porsche.  
She’s a super model.  He’s a hairy biker…
2.  BELIEFS and OPINIONS. These are the hero’s 
VALUES.  Things like: Abortion is a sin. The military keeps 
America free. Rich people make their money by exploiting the 
workers. That girl is out of my league.   
3.  PRIMAL ESSENCE. When the hero is under intense 
pressure, fighting for his life, his beliefs and opinions don’t matter 
anymore. He goes beyond intellect and operates out of pure 
primal drives.  
This reveals psychological values the hero didn’t know he or 
she had.
For Example,
A Story Sequence:

a) Late night in a restaurant. The last customer is a 
neurosurgeon who insults the Mexican busboy, “Why don’t you go 
back to your own country? We don’t need more people like you 
here.”
  
b) A half hour later, driving down the highway, the 
neurosurgeon sees a school bus careen into a ditch and bust into 
flames. He stops to help but he’s afraid of the flames. He know he 
should go in and help the kids, but he can’t bring himself to do it.
c) The Mexican busboy in his beat up truck, who is driving 
home, sees the accident and stops. He goes in the burning bus, 
starts rescuing kids, handing them, one-by-one, to the doctor. 
They’re a team, saving lives.
d) Under pressure, we see the Mexican busboy is braver 
than the Doc. At his core, he’s a better person. Shamed by the 
Mexican, the doctor forces himself to risk his life and help the 
kids, too. 
Under pressure, we strip away all of the Hero’s the surface 
characteristics, as well as his or her beliefs and opinions, and we 
see who the character REALLY is at their primal core.  
How to Develop Depth:
1.  At the beginning of your story, you can use 
characteristics. You don’t have to. If you’re Tennessee Williams, 
writing a play, you start right out with values. 

2.  You soon move to beliefs and opinions. You can’t create 
character with just characteristics. (Novice writers try to do this; “a 
biker who recites Shakespeare.”)
3.  During the rising conflict section, the hero starts 
questioning his cliche beliefs. He asks, “How did I get myself into 
this?” 
It’s one thing to have beliefs an opinions in the comfort of 
your living room. Like being an armchair quarterback. The whole 
point of a story is to put the hero under so much pressure he has 
to CONFRONT his beliefs and opinions in the deepest way. He’s 
forced to look at his unexamined values.
4.  Battle sequence. The hero is worn down, exhausted.  
The pressure has made him forget his beliefs, and forget 
intellectualizing. He’s beyond that. He has to push forward on raw 
will and courage. Passion, primal drives.  
5.  Self Revelation.  The hero confronts his own values.  He 
realizes what he has based his entire life on. He attains a lucid 
state where he sees all his beliefs laid out on a table. 
At this point, he either transcends (changes his deepest 
values) or is destroyed (is unable to change his values).
Examples:
First Blood (1982, held the record for most tickets sold until 
2018).  The self revelation scene was a siloquoy where Rambo 
confronts who he is and what made him that way.

Do the Right Thing. Danny Aeillo character, under pressure 
calls the neighborhood kids niggers. Revealing another white guy 
who’s racist at his core.
6. New Equillibrium.  At the very end, the hero does 
something to show that he’s a changed person. 
In light stories, sometimes there’s a return to the hero’s 
characteristics (Tootsie).
V. The Hero’s Ghost:
Definition of GHOST:
The “ghost” is a specific thing that happened to the hero in 
the backstory. Something disturbing from his past. An event that 
still haunts him. The ghost has twisted the Hero’s values – and is 
casting a shadow over the hero’s entire life.  
For example,
Airplane! (1980 film, won Best Screenplay)
Back in Viet Nam, the Hero had to land a cargo plane on a 
battlefield runway, but crashed and the crew was killed. 
Now it’s years later, he’s a passenger on a commercial jet. 
When the captain and crew get food poisoning, the hero is the 
only one who can land the plane. 
The hero is terrified – because he sees history repeating 
itself.

For example:
The Patriot — The hero won’t fight the English tyrants 
because he acted cruelly in a battle in the past, and never wants 
to repeat the edxperience.
The GHOST is the source of the hero’s problems.  The 
source of his insecurity.  It’s the key to developing his INTERNAL 
CONFLICTS.  
The hero’s GHOST tells him, “You can’t do this…  You’re not 
good enough… You’re not worthy… You’re going to fail…”
How to Develop Ghost:
1.  The Backstory opening.  A “prologue” scene (or 
sequence) that shows something terrible happen to the hero, and 
how the hero acquired his GHOST.
2. If you open the story with a “Hero’s ghost sequence,” the 
audience knows what the hero’s ghost is from the start, and you 
can use this to get DRAMATIC IRONY.
(what is v, what should be; what’s said v what’s meant; 
    As the hero embarks on his desire (land the plane and 
save the passengers), the audience knows the hero has 
something extra to lose. The hero has a deep psychological risk. 
If he fails a second time, he’ll never be able to recover.  

3.  OR… the hero’s GHOST can be hidden from the 
audience. If you hold back this info, then you tell the audience 
something’s wrong with the hero, but not what it is. 
This builds MYSTERY.  “What wrong with him?  What’s 
holding him back?”
4. You can show the hero’s GHOST in a flashback scene or 
sequence. This answers the question, “What’s wrong with the 
hero,” and it solves that MYSTERY.
Or … You can write a scene in which the hero TELLS what 
his ghost is (instead of showing it).  
5. Once you REVEAL the hero’s ghost, you can get 
DRAMATIC IRONY.  For example, Hero sees something that 
reminds him of his ghost. When we, the audience, watch him see 
the thing, we know how it affects him.
IRONY: just when the hero must be at his strongest, we see 
a tangible manifestation of the psychological ghost weakening 
him. 
6. Once you’ve explained what the hero’s GHOST is, you 
can refer to it in on-going flashbacks – this is a device to show 
how the GHOST is psychologicaly debilitating the Hero.  
7. SELF REVELATION. The Hero’s self revelation either —
a) kills the ghost, (Hero transcends)
b) the ghost kills him, (Hero is destroyed)

c) or (in a comedy) the Hero and the ghost find a way to live 
together (The Ghost and Mrs. Muir).
CONCLUSION:
 
Change, Desire, Motive, Depth, and Ghost are the FIVE 
possible dimensions of your hero. There are no other 
possibilities.  
I’ve separated these into five “dimensions.”  This is a bit 
artificial. In a living character, all these dimensions are mixing 
together, cross-referencing, playing off each other to create a 
whole person.
I’ve separated them because you have to learn how to 
control each dimension, one at a time. You have to gain skill 
developing each one.  
When you’re writing, and you feel something is wrong with 
your hero, if you think something is missing, these are the only 
possible things you can do. Go through these steps and you can 
see where your hero is under developed.  
Don’t try to put all of these dimensions into each scene.  
These have to be paced. Aspects of the hero are placed here and 
there. Organizing these dimensions is like composing music.

Character 
Part 3
TYPES OF CHARACTERS
In stories, there are only SEVEN kinds of characters – no other 
possibilities.  They are:
Heroes
Opponents
Allies.
Foils.
Minions
Storytellers.
Commentators.
Of these, Heroes, Opponents and Allies are the three most 
important. 
The Storyteller is the next most important type (especially in 
a novel.)
Foils, minions and commentators are the least.
OPPONENTS:  
The Main Opponent:
The first and most important issue in any story is, “Who 
fights whom over what?”  This is the CENTRAL ISSUE in any 
story.

The Hero and the Main Opponent take opposite sides over 
this single issue. 
Writers have to find the single, central issue – or they have 
no story.
KEY:
The Hero and the Main Opponent must want the same 
thing.
Examples:
It’s a Wonderful Life: George Bailey and Mr. Potter both 
want their vision to control the town.
Rambo: Rambo and the Police both want to control Rambo’s 
freedom.
Frankie and Johnny: Both Johnny and Frankie want to 
have true love. 
Raiders of the Lost Ark: Both Indiana and the Nazis want 
the Lost Ark of the Covenent.
The Hero and the Main Opponent are locked in a battle to 
the death over a single, central issue. 
In some stories, locking the Hero and Opponent together is 
easy.

For example,
Rocky. Only one person can win the fight. They both want to 
win. They’re locked in.
BUT in other stories you have to explain the “unity of 
opposites.”  Explain WHY the Hero and the Main Opponent are in 
the same place, fighting over the single issue.
For example,
The Wizard of Oz.  Why does the Wicked Witch want to kill 
Dorothy? Because Dorothy’s house landed on the Witch’s sister.  
Otherwise, there would be no reason for them to fight. You have 
to explain why they’re locked into combat. 
The Hero’s Double:
The Main Opponent is the most important character in your 
story next to the hero. But the story is NOT about the Opponent, 
it’s about the Hero.
You have to use the Opponent to develop the Hero.
Technique:
Make the Main Opponent both the SAME and DIFFERENT 
from the Hero.
SAME: the opponent is in some way the DOUBLE of the 
Hero. 

For example,
Rocky.  Apollo is another fighter competing for the same 
title.
It’s a Wonderful Life. Potter is another banker trying to 
organize the same town according to his agenda.
This enables the audience to COMPARE the Hero and the 
Main Opponent. (Comparison / Contrast = Definition.)
DIFFERENT: the Main Opponent has VALUES that oppose 
the Hero — this is so the audience can see which way of life is 
superior. 
The Hero and the Main Opponent have different motives for 
wanting to win. 
Example:
Rocky.  Rocky fight because he wants to prove to himself 
that he has integrity and he’s not just another bum. To Apollo, the 
fight is just business. 
It’s a Wonderful Life. George Bailey wants people in the 
town to live decent lives with optimism. Potter wants to suck the 
money out the townspeople to enrich himself.

The Main Opponent must be as powerful (or appear to be 
more powerful) than the Hero. The Hero and the Main Opponent 
drive each other to greatness.  
2-Point Opposition:
Some stories (or short stories) use 2-Point Opposition. 
HERO ———> OPPONENT
The only way to develop interesting characters in a story like 
this is to give the Opponent the same amount of complexity as the 
Hero.
Love stories (Crossing Delancy) and buddy stories (Midnight 
Run) are genres built to do this. They develop two characters to a 
high degree.
PLAYS (because of budget) often have only two characters. 
(They’re called “Two-Handers.”) Sleuth. The Owl and the 
Pussycat. Zoo Story. Educating Rita.)
To get complexity,
Both the Hero, the Opponent are a multi-dimensional 
characters. They both have CHANGE, a (counter) DESIRE, has 
MOTIVATIONS based on a VLAUE SYSTEM, he has DEPTH.
It’s rare that the OPPONENT has a GHOST. 
Instead of a Ghost, he has a FLAW that causes him to act 
wrongly towards others. 

For example,
Rocky. Apollo’s FLAW: he believes fighting is just show 
business.
It’s a Wonderful Life. Potter’s FLAW is his greed.
The way to create a MORE SOPHISTICATED story is to 
build a CHARACTER WEB.
The first way to do this is to have SECONDARY 
OPPONENTS (you’ve probably noticed that most stories have 
more than one opponent.)  
4-POINT OPPOSITION:
4-Point opposition looks like this:

HERO
A
C
B

Four characters whose value systems are all opposed to 
each other.  
4-Point opposition is a HUGELY important technique. ALL 
sophisticated writing uses it.  Long stories (like TV shows) have to 
use it. 
This is the backbone of TONS of genres, novels, film, TV.  
For example:
FRASIER: Frasier, Niles, Martin, Daphne (at home). Frasier, 
Roz, The Boss, Bulldog (at work).
DICK VAN DYKE SHOW. Rob, Laura, Millie, Jerry (at home). 
Rob, Sally, Buddy, Richard Deacon (at work).
LOST: Kate, Holloway, Fox, John Locke. 
Four point opposition enables you to imply a complete 
society.
Once you know what to look for, 4-Point Opposition is in 
stories everywhere — it’s how you build sophisticated stories. 
4-Point Opposition is SUPER IMPORTANT.  It’s a mjor 
subject in advanced writing. For now, we’ll talk about how to use it 
to build a WEB of OPPONENTS.  

This is 4-Point Opposition to the HERO— the Hero is opposed to 
the MAIN OPPONENT (A), and two secondary opponents (B & 
C). He faces a WEB of opponents.
The PURPOSE of any opponent is to develop an aspect of 
the Hero.
The Main Opponent has one way of attacking the Hero. You 
give each Secondary Opponent a different way of attacking the 
Hero.
Example:
Smokey and the Bandit.

Main Opponent: Sheriff Justice. He’s on the Bandit’s tail 
trying to stop him from delivering the beer, and develops how the

2nd Opponent: Carrie. She wants the Bandit to be her 
boyfriend, and develops how he’s a loner whose never been able 
to settle down.
3rd Opponent: Enos. He develops how the Bandit is a

Note that, in addition to opposing the Hero, each opponent 
opposes the other opponents. 
(Smokey and the Bandit has good structure.  Quality writing 
of a hokey idea.) 
Opponents shouldn’t just oppose the Hero’s DESIRE. Each 
one should uniquely oppose the hero’s VALUES. 
Each character in the web of opposition has a value system 
that contrasts part of the Hero’s value system.
Examples:
American Beauty (1999 film).  Hero, Wife, Son, Colonel

King Lear. Lear, Cordelia, Gonerail, Regan. 
The Godfarther: Godfather, Sonny, Fredo, Michael.

RULE OF OPPOSITION!
Create EXTREME characters.
Weak writers try to get story interest by creating extreme 
situations.  No!  Create extreme characters – and they will create 
extreme situations, organically.  
For Example,
The Odd Couple. 
Oscar, the biggest slob in the world.
Felix, the most neurotic neatnik in the world.
THE ALLY:
This is a character who HELPS the Hero. Often has 
knowledge (or a skill) the Hero needs. He’s also propinquity for 
the hero. One good way to explain the Hero’s thoughts and 
values is to create an ALLY. 
Usually, the Ally has the same goal as the Hero. But he may 
have a goal of his own.
There are THREE ways to write an ally character. The 
FIRST is the true ally. Someone who sincerely wants to help the 
hero.

For example,
The Wizard of Oz. The Scarecrow, the Tin Man.  
Don Quixote. Sancho Panza. 
SECOND, 
The Opponent —> Ally:
This is a character who appears at first to be an OPPONENT 
of the Hero, but turns into an ALLY. 
For Example,
The Wizard of Oz. The Cowardly Lion.
Rocky. Mick, Rocky’s trainer. 
Three Days of the Condor. Kathy Hale, the woman.
THIRD,
The Ally —> Opponent:  
This is a character who appears to be the Hero’s friend but 
whom we discover is really an OPPONENT.
Use this character to give the Main Opponent power. 
Is a complex and interesting character – is usually torn by 
the dilemma. While pretending to be a friend of the Hero, he or 

she comes to feel like a genuine ally.  Even though they know 
they are working against him. 
This Ally’s internal conflict peaks when he or she is revealed.  
For example:  
The Verdict. The Hero’s girlfriend is really a spy for the Main 
Opponent. 
NOTE: one or more of the opponents can have allies, too.

MINIONS:
The multitudes. The crowd scenes. The mob. 
You use minions to show the SCOPE or ACTION of a

THE STORYTELLER 
This is the character who tells or recounts the story, 
usually (but not always) the Hero. 
Sometimes the hero is looking back (Waltons)
Sometimes the hero tells his thinking process as he pursues 
an opponent, and provides transitions (detective stories)
Have to decide what type of insight the STORYTELLER is 
going to give. 
Sometimes the Storyteller is a major character aside from 
the Hero (Amadeus)
Sometimes a minor character (Mad Max)
Sometimes it’s an epic storyteller (The Odyssey, The Ten

Sometimes you have multiple storytellers (Citizen Kane)
Storytellers are used in novels more than in screenplays. In

The COMMENTATOR in modern stories is almost always a

ARCHETYPES
Archetypes are characters that play basic roles within 
society.  Because these social roles are common to all people, 
they cross cultural boundaries and have universal appeal.
Using an archetype can give a character the appearance of 
weight and depth quickly.

The audience recognizes these types of characters – but 
archetypes are a BLUNT TOOL. Unless you give them detail, 
they’re a stereotype. The key to doing this is to develop both the 
POSITIVE and NEGATIVE sides of the character.
Here’s a list of archetypes that have universal appeal:
King or Father:
Positive: 
He leads his community (or family) with wisdom and resolve 
so they can succeed and grow.
Negative: 
Can be a tyrant.  Force his people to act according to a strict 
and oppressive rules.  Can be emotionally cold.
Examples: King Arthur. The Godfather. Casablanca. Lord of 
the Rings. Citizen Kane.
Queen or Mother:
Positive:
Provides care and a protective shell within which the child 
(or the people) can grow.
Negative:
Can be a tyrant. Can use guilt and shame to keep the child 
close for her own selfish comfort. Can cripple a child by being 
overly protective.
Examples: The Lion in Winter. The Glass Menagerie.  

The Wise Old One (The Wizard):
Positive:
Passes on knowledge and wisdom to help the hero (or the 
community).
Negative:
May want to force people to think his way. Wants glory for 
himself, not for the sake of knowledge.
Examples: Yoda in Star Wars. Hannibal in Silence of the 
Lambs. Gandalf in Lord of the Rings. 
The Warrior:
Positive:
The practical enforcer of what is “right.” 
Negative:
Lives by the motto: “Might Makes Right.” What is weak gets 
destroyed – so becomes the enforcer of what is “wrong.”
Examples: Achilles in The Iliad. Luke Skywalker in Star 
Wars. Patton. Die Hard. The Terminator.
The Magician:
Positive:
Can make hidden reality visible. Can balance and control the 
forces of nature.

Negative:
Can manipulate nature to enslave people and destroy the 
harmony of the world.  
Examples: Harry Potter. Merlin.  Gandalf in Lord of the 
Rings.  A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur’s Court.  Sherlock 
Holmes. 
The Trickster:
Positive:
Uses confidence, trickery and a way with words to get what 
he wants.
Negative:
Can become a con man who gains at the expense of others.
Dexamples: Odysseus in The Odyssey. Beverly Hills Cop.  
Crocodile Dundee. Sgt. Bilko. Tootsie. Huckleberry Finn.  
The Artist:
Positive: 
Defines excellence for people. Shows them beauty and a 
vision of the future.
Negative: 
Is the ultimate fascist, insisting on perfection. Wants a world 
where everything can be controlled. May want to tear everything 
down out of spite.  

Examples: Achilles in The Iliad. Frankenstein. Hamlet.  
Holden Caulfield in Catcher in the Rye.
The Lover:
Positive: 
Provides the care, understanding and sensuality that can 
make someone a complete person.
Negative: 
Can lose himself in the other. Can force the other to stand in 
his or her shadow.  Or forces the other to lose him or herself in 
him.
Examples: Romeo and Juliette. Wuthering Heights.  
Casablanca. Frankie and Johnny. Crossing Delancy. 
The Rebel:
Positive: 
Has the courage to act against the system that is enslaving 
people.
Negative:  Cannot provide a better alternative, so ends up 
harming the system or a community.
Examples:  American Beauty.  Hamlet.  Casablanca.  Rebel 
Without a Cause.  Reds.  

The Searcher
Positive:
Always questioning, always growing.
Negative:
Often purposless, often uncommitted. Uses the quest to not 
take a stand.
The Martyr:
Positive:
A charscter who will take a stand.  To make a better life 
possible for pweople.  They will take the blows.
Negative:
This char enslaves other people.  By setting up opportunities 
to take blows.  Will often create an opportunity to be beat on so 
they can blame someone else.  Very theatrical. 
The Teacher:
Positive:
Someone who passes on knowledge so poeple can Iive 
better. 
Negative:
This character forces students to think a certain way.  Looks 
for self glorification instead of glory of the ideas.

THE THREE TYPES OF HERO
The HERO who has a moral deficiency at the beginning of a 
story, pursues a DESIRE, encounters opposition, and changes his 
VALUES at the end, is the TRADITIONAL hero.
This is the kind of HERO we’ve been talking about, and is 
the most common kind of hero. 
But there are TWO other ways to write a hero.  The first of 
these is THE SERIAL HERO.
The Serial Hero:
The Serial Hero has deep PSYCHOLOGICAL needs at the 
beginning of the story. 
He has little or no MORAL need, and whether or not he has 
MORAL FLAWS doesn’t even come into question. 
At the end of the story, the Serial Hero does NOT undergo a 
moral change.
Examples:
Indiana Jones.  James Bond.  Axel Foley in Beverly Hills 
Cop.  Charles Bronson in Death Wish.  Rambo.  Captain Kirk.  

The Serial Hero is the staple of episodic television.
The SERIAL HERO may go through extreme angst in an 
episodic story, but at the end, you have to return the Hero to the 
status quo. 
Examples:
Captain Kirk.  
He’s a wandering explorer whose mission is to seek out 
strange new worlds. He wishes he could settle down with a family 
and have a ranch and a horse, but he can’t. For him, it’s duty 
before love. No matter what happens, he’ll be setting off for a new 
mission at the end of every episode.
Question:
How do you develop a SERIAL HERO if he can’t undergo a 
moral change?
Answer:
There are Two Ways to develop a Serial Hero:
First,
Test his Character.  Give him obstacles of different kinds to 
overcome. Give him fascinating challenges.
For example,
Capatin Kirk in Star Trek is a Captain of a team. He has to 
use intelligence, nerves of steel, cleverness, and the unique 
talents of his crew to save his ship. 

Beverly Hills Cop is a TRICKSTER. We’re entertained by 
how he maneuvers.  
McGiver is a mechanical genius. We’re entertained by how 
he gets himself out of problems. 
Sherlock Holes is the world’s most perceptive intellectual 
genius. We’re entertained by how he thinks his way to great 
achievements.
Second,
Take the SERIAL HERO to the brink of a powerful self 
revelation. He fully realizes who he is and how he acts towards 
other people – but makes a conscious decision to NOT change.  
The HREO sees no option to be any other way.
This DEEPENS his character.
City On the Edge of Forever.
Captain Kirk is forced to let the woman he loves die – to 
save galactic history. It’s an experience he will never get over. Yet 
he knows he did the “right” thing.  
As a result, he’s more a dedicated star ship captain than 
ever before. 
Since the Serial Hero can’t see any better way to be, it’s up 
to the AUDIENCE to decide if he or she is “MORAL.” Or has 
improved his life. Or is making the right choice.  

The hero, himself, sees no alternative.
Example:  Death Wish. (A story about vigilante justice.) The 
HERO sees no better way to be. So, the audience must ask, “Is 
the HERO a moral person?” “Has he been doing the right thing?” 
“Should he continue blowing people away for justice?” 
This was a heated discussion when the movie came out. 
There were 4 sequels.
THE TRAVELING ANGEL:
In the TRAVELING ANGEL story, the HERO is an absolutely 
PERFECT character.
The question of whether they are acting MORALLY or 
IMMORALLY never enters their mind.  The HERO is perfect, and 
he knows it.
This hero is called the TRAVELING ANGEL because he 
travels from place to place. At the beginning of the story, he 
comes into the community.  At the end of the story, he leaves 
again.
Often, this HERO appears to spend his entire life traveling 
from one community to another.

Examples:
Mary Poppins.  Ferris Beuler’s Day Off.  One Flew Over the 
Cuckoo’s Nest.  The Christ Story.  The Music Man.  Our Man 
Godfrey.  The Seven Faces of Dr Lao. Sherlock Holmes. Dr. 
Richard Kimball in the Fugitive (TV).
When the TRAVELING ANGEL enters a community, he or 
she disrupts the balance of forces in it.  
He usually follows horizontal form, going from place to place 
in the community, encountering allies and opponents. 
There is a series of sequences in this kind of story in which 
the HERO meets someone, we see that person’s moral flaw, and 
the HERO rudely and bluntly exposes their flaw. 
The hero is brutally honest and brilliantly perceptive.
He forces a series of characters to confront their own 
contradictions and hypocrisies – which usually makes people 
angry.
The people in the community have two reactions to the 
TRAVELING ANGEL: 
a) Perceptive people are fascinated by the HERO’s 
perfectness AND,  
b) Other people, who have settled into living a hypocritical 
life, are INSULTED and ANNOYED.  

Often, in the Battle Sequence of the TRAVELING ANGEL 
story, a faction in community tries to kill the hero – because he’s 
caused so much trouble.
If the hero survives, it’s because enough people in the 
community realize he is right about them. 
The BATTLE SEQUENCE ends with a series of people 
coming to the realization the hero has been right about THEIR 
moral flaws. And they decide to change. 
You’ve set up the moral needs of the people in the 
community – here you knock them down like dominoes.
The TRAVELING ANGEL offers the townspeople a new way 
of life, then blows out of town again.
The hero has NO SELF REVELATION. He’s perfect to begin 
with and he’s perfect at the end.
The SELF REVELATIONS goes to the various characters in 
the community.
CONCLUSION:
Each of these THREE kinds of HERO use the MORAL 
FLAW to SELF REVELATION to NEW EQUILLIBRIUM steps in a 
different way

The TRADITIONAL hero has a moral flaw, then a self 
revelation and the end, leading to a new equilibrium.
The SERIAL hero explicitly decides not to change his life 
because he can see no better way to be. Whether he is right or 
wrong is left up to the audience. 
The TRAVELING ANGEL is a perfect character with no 
moral flaw, at all.  And the MORAL FLAW to SELF REVELATION 
to NEW EQUILLIBRIUM is given to the various characters in the 
community.
