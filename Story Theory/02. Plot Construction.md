## Definition of a Story

A story consists of four essential elements:

1. **Hero** — Every story is about a central character.
2. **Desire** — The hero wants something.
3. **Conflict** — Another force opposes the hero’s desire.
4. **Change** — The struggle changes the hero’s values in a deep and lasting way.

A piece of writing that contains all four elements is a story.

If one or more are missing, the result may be a vignette, sketch, anecdote, parable, flash fiction, or monologue. Note: these are all short. 
STORY enables you to sustain interest for a long time. 

## HOW TO CREATE A STORY

When you read a book or watch a show, what is it that you notice?

	ACTION.

	DIALOGUE.

	Exciting SUBJECT MATERIAL.

	STYLE or DESCRIPTION.

But NONE of these carry the story.  

What DOES create story?

Story is the ARRANGEMENT of your SCENES.  It’s the organization of the events.  It’s putting the right chunks in the right order.

Scene arrangement is like the concrete and steel of a skyscraper. 

Action, dialogue, description are the window dressing.

Even though it’s the most important thing, structure is also the most INVISIBLE.

Audiences aren’t aware of structure.  It’s ALSO the part that writers tend to know the LEAST about.

    ## COMMON STORY MISTAKES

    Since most writers don’t understand how to structure stories, they make fundamental mistakes in the way they APPROACH writing a story.  Here’s some of of the most common ones:

    ### Too Much Action:

This writer thinks, “I’m going to give them excitement.  This story’s never going to let up.  I’m going to give them one great scene after another.”

The problems with too much action:

No pacing.  It wears the audience out.

No chance to get to know who the characters are in an interesting way.

Too much of one kind of conflict.  Gets boring fast.

Explosions and drive-by shootings and sex scenes are not interesting unless there’s interesting context.

### Too Many Jokes:

This writer thinks he’s got a fantastic sense of humor.  This story is going to be hysterical.
The problem is, sight gags & clever dialogue don’t create a story.  In fact, jokes usually KILL a story.

Jokes work by creating an expectation in the audience’s mind then delivering a punch line that takes you to an absurd image.

“I just flew in from Chicago and 
boy, are my arms tired.”

If you try to plot by going into absurd, your story becomes spaced out. There are ways to put jokes in a story if you know how to construct a story.  But you can’t create a coherent story out of jokes. 

An audience can’t relate to characters and situations that are absurd for long.

### The Verisimilitude Story

This writer thinks that writing exactly what happens in real life is interesting.  

The hero talks with his father.  Then he puts his kids to bed.  Then he talks to his buddy in the bar. Then he skips some rocks on a lake.  All this is punctuated by the wind in the trees.

Real life is not that interesting.  That’s why people read books and watch shows.  Writing “real life” is not writing truth that anyone is interested in.  

Good stories distill the truth.  Stories aren’t about the real world — they’re about the STORY world.

### Storyline Too Simplistic

Some stories do the basic things a story is “supposed” to do, but they’re boring.

Another cop vs. serial killer story.  
	(guns, car chases, a sicko killer — still boring)

Another battle between galactic empires.
	(space ships, aliens, explosions — still boring)

A lot of writers know that a story needs:

	A hero
	An Opponent
	Conflict
	A change in the hero’s values

So they do it — but the story is still dull.

Because they DON’T have an INTERESTING  structure.

Audiences today are sophisticated.  It’s NOT enough to know you need a hero, a villain and rising conflict. It’s NOT enough to know how to write a so-called “correct arc.”  

You have to take your stories and characters to places that will interest the audience. To do that, you need a higher level of craft today than ever before.
