## DEFINITION OF A STORY

A story consists of four essential elements:

1. **Hero** — Every story is about a central character.
2. **Desire** — The hero wants something.
3. **Conflict** — Another force opposes the hero’s desire.
4. **Change** — The struggle changes the hero’s values in a deep and lasting way.

A piece of writing that contains all four elements is a story.

If one or more are missing, what you have is often a vignette, sketch, anecdote, parable, flash fiction, or monologue. Note: these are all short forms. A STORY is what can hold an audience's interest over a long stretch of time -- the length of a novel, a movie, a television season, or in the case of a long running soap opera, even decades.

---

## HOW TO CREATE A STORY

When you read a book or watch a show, what you notice is:

- ACTION.

- DIALOGUE.

- Exciting SUBJECT MATERIAL.

- STYLE or DESCRIPTION.

But NONE of these carry the story. What then DOES create story?

Story comes from how you ARRANGE your SCENES. It’s the organization of the events. It’s putting the right chunks in the right order. 

Think of it this way. Scene arrangement is like the concrete and steel of a skyscraper. Action, dialogue and description are the window dressing.

Oddly enough, even though it’s the most important aspect of story, structure is the most INVISIBLE. Audiences aren’t aware of structure, and it’s ALSO the part that writers tend to know the LEAST about.

---

## COMMON STORY MISTAKES

Since most writers don’t understand structure, they build flaws into their stories they can't recover from. This happens when writers approach writing a story in a fundamentally wrong way. Here’s some of the most common flawed approaches:


### Too Much Action:

Non-stop action exhausts the audience. They never get a moment to get emotionally invested. This writer thinks, “I’m going to give them excitement. This story’s never going to let up.  I’m going to give them one great scene after another.” But that's not what happens.  

Here's what goes wrong with too much action:

- No pacing. It wears the audience out.

- No time to develop interesting characters the audience to cares about.

- Too much of one kind of conflict gets boring fast.

- Explosions, drive-by shootings and sex scenes aren't inherently interesting. They need a meaningful context.


### Too Many Jokes:

This writer thinks he’s got a great sense of humor. This story is going to be hilarious.
The problem is, humor by itself doesn't create story. In fact, jokes usually KILL a story.

Jokes generally work by taking you into the absurd. For example:

“I just flew in from Chicago and boy, are my arms tired.”

"I want to die peacefully in my sleep like my grandfather, not screaming like the passengers in his car."

If you try to plot by going into this absurd like this, your story loses coherence. There are ways to weave jokes into your story -- but first you have to know how to construct a story. You can't build a coherent story by stringing jokes together.

Absurd characters and situations can't hold an audience for long.


### Too Much Verisimilitude 

Some writers try to recreate life exactly as it happens, believing that realism creates drama.

The hero talks with his father. He puts his kids to bed. He talks to his bartender. He skips rocks on a lake. All this is punctuated by the wind in the trees.

Real life is not interesting. That’s why people read books and watch shows. Just because something is true, doesn't mean it's also interesting. 

Stories aren’t about the real world — they’re about the STORY world. Good stories distill the truth. 


### Storyline Too Simplistic

Some writers think they can make an ordinary story interesting simply by choosing sensational subject matter.

Another detective hunting a serial killer.
Another battle between galactic empires.
Another zombie apocalypse.
Another vampire romance.
Another spy trying to stop World War III.

Sensational subject matter doesn't create interest. Story structure does.

Many writers know that a story needs these basic elements:

A hero
An opponent
Conflict
A change in the hero’s values

They put these elements in — and wonder why the story is still dull. It's because they DON’T have an INTERESTING  structure.

Audiences today are sophisticated. It's not enough to know you need a hero, a villain, and conflict. It's not enough to know how to write a so-called "correct arc." You have to take your story somewhere the audience hasn't been before. That requires a higher level of craft than ever before.

## TRADITIONAL DRAMA FORM

The traditional drama has nine basic steps. Let's examine each one.

### 1.  HERO’S MORAL FLAW

At the beginning of your story, the hero acts IMMORALLY towards other characters in your story. The hero is NOT aware of his moral FLAW.

Definitions:

MORALITY = how a character acts towards other people. In stories, “morality” exists only in how characters interact, how they treat each other. There’s no such thing as a “moral” or “immoral” thought.

PSYCHOLOGY = how a character acts towards himself. In stories, a character's “psychology” refers to his or her internal conflicts.


#### EXAMPLE:  A Christmas Carol

<img width="250" height="285" alt="xmas carol" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/961a7065-12cf-4ab5-b9a4-0bb2c18e95e7" />

Scrooge is the hero. His MORAL flaw is that he’s a miser -- and he inflicts his miserly values onto the other characters. He won’t pay Cratchet enough. He won’t let him have Christmas off.  

If Scrooge was just a miser in his house, by himself, obsessively counting up his money, it wouldn’t be a moral flaw. It could be a psychological flaw. But what makes Scrooge “immoral” is that he inflicts his nasty values onto the other characters.  

Note that Scrooge is NOT AWARE he’s acting immorally. He thinks he’s acting sensibly — and that everyone else needs to see the world the way HE does.


#### EXAMPLE: Rocky

<img width="250" height="285" alt="rocky" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/762b4f86-8e96-4b67-adca-e44c38b2feb1" />

The hero, Rocky, under confident. He allows other characters in the story to convince him that he’s a loser. He buys in to feeling inadequate. He believes others when they tell him he’s a bum and will never amount to anything. 

This is an example of a passive moral need.
<p>&nbsp;</p>

### 2.  HERO’S UNSATISFACTORY LIFE

The hero lives an unsatisfying life. He senses something is missing, but he doesn't understand what it is, or why. His low quality life is caused by his moral flaw, but he's completely unaware of this. He doesn’t know that he has a moral flaw.  

#### EXAMPLE: The Wizard of Oz

<img width="250" height="285" alt="Wiz" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/7e5ce8d1-bd2b-45cc-8c14-0ad827c5b759" />

Dorothy believes the farm is her enemy. She blames her problems on being stuck there, and imagines that she could only be happy somewhere else, far away, over the rainbow. Getting to such a place is impossible, of course, so she’s stuck in a rotten life.  

#### EXAMPLE: Rocky

<img width="250" height="285" alt="rocky 2" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/5ecbb672-31f8-45c1-87bf-73e3f6e57de1" />

Rocky lives in a crummy neighborhood where nobody respects him. He works for a loan shark. He fights in sleazy clubs. He berates himself. He’s vaguely dissatisfied, but he doesn’t know see a way to improve his life. He's stuck.
<p>&nbsp;</p>


### 3.  THE INCITING INCIDENT

An event occurs that forces the hero to go from a vaguely unsatisfactory life to having a specific desire. This is NOT something the hero chooses. It is  ALWAYS something that happens to the Hero.   

The inciting event causes the hero to do something he or she has never done before. This is the event that launches the story.

#### EXAMPLE: The Wizard of Oz

<img width="250" height="285" alt="Wiz" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/c77f8489-bf32-4b97-805b-a089102e3958" />

Miss Gulch comes to the farmhouse to take Toto, Dorothy’s dog, to make sure it’s destroyed. This forces Dorothy to run away from home, something she’s never done before.

#### EXAMPLE: Rocky

<img width="250" height="285" alt="rocky" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/1d57cff8-bc08-4647-a18e-599961334280" />

Apollo Creed opens a book of fighters and picks one called “The Italian Stallion” because he likes the name. When Rocky is told about it, he doesn't think he's worthy, but he is FORCED to agree to the fight. 

Note that before the Inciting Incident, the hero has a crummy life, but he’s getting through it more or less. The Inciting Incident is an event that upsets his world — and it causes him to do something he’s never done before. 

The Inciting Incident is not something the Hero chooses. It's always something that just happens to him, either by accident, or because some other character in the story makes a decision about him.
<p>&nbsp;</p>


### 4. THE HERO'S DESIRE

The hero’s DESIRE is something the hero wants to possess or achieve. It’s a sudden goal caused by the Inciting Incident. For example:

- In a love story, the boy wants the girl.  

- In a detective story, the investigator wants to catch the criminal.  

- In a war story, the general wants to capture the territory.  

- In a adventure story, the hero wants to find the lost treasure. 

In every story, the hero will have one MAIN desire. He or she may have lesser desires as well, so these goals need to be organized. The first way to do this is to build a DESIRE LINE. A Desire Line is a chain of goals that builds from low to high, leading toward the hero's main desire.
Building to the MAIN Desire Line:

At the beginning of your story, construct a chain of desires that build from LOW to HIGH, or from less powerful to more powerful.  

#### EXAMPLE:  Adventure Story

<img width="250" height="285" alt="adventure" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/ba5da74a-52ce-44d9-8929-49214911dc95" />

This is the WRONG way to start the hero’s MAIN desire line: In Scene 1, someone hands the hero a map and says, “There’s a million dollars in treasure right here, where it says X.” This is weak writing, because the writer lost a lot of intrinsically interesting material.

Here’s a BETTER way to BUILD to the hero’s main desire:

Open on a chase through a crowded city street. A panicked man is clutching an envelope. He barely reaches a mailbox and shoves it inside. Seconds later, he's stabbed to death.

- The mysterious envelope arrives at tsomeone’s house. The HERO, a college professor, finds it, tosses it onto his desk without a second thought, and ignores it. This creates suspense.

- Later, the hero idly opens the envelope. Inside is an ancient ring and a handwritten note: "Help me."

- Mystified, the hero researches the ring at the library and discovers it's an ancient Egyptian scarab.

- The hero visits an elderly professor, who recognizes the ring immediately. He explains that it was stolen decades ago under mysterious circumstances. The professor pulls a stack of dusty books from the shelf and explains the research he abandoned years ago. The ring could only have come from the lost city of King Tut.

- The professor hands the hero a map, ans ays, "At the center of this lost city lies the treasure of King Tut.”

This is how you build a Desire Line. Start with a low desire, then move through a series of increasingly powerful desires until you reach the hero's main desire. You now have what every writer needs: SIX scenes. 
<p>&nbsp;</p>


### 5.  The Opponent

An Opponent is a character who tries to keep the Hero from attaining his desire. The MAIN opponent is the most important character in your story next to the Hero. (Some writers claim the opponent is the most important character, of all.)

The Opponent wants the same thing as the Hero. Therefore, the opponent and the hero can’t peacefully coexist. One of them has to be either TRANSFORMED, or DESTROYED. 

#### EXAMPLE: Rocky

<img width="250" height="285" alt="rocky" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/a2754eca-9209-4e81-b59f-334642d054b0" />

The main opponent is Apollo Creed. Rocky and Apollo are fighting for the same title. Only one of them can win it.

#### EXAMPLE: Raiders of the Lost Ark

<img width="250" height="285" alt="raiders" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/3e82ba79-94ca-4b70-8db8-0de3b1d0fdbf" />

Indiana Jones want to find the Lost Ark so he can put it in a museum where it can be studied by scholars around the world. The opponent, a German archeologist wants the ark to help the Nazi war effort. Only one of them can have it.

Notice this is not just two characters fighting over the Ark, but two completely different ways of life. The heo reprents civilization and culture; the opponent reprents power, war and destruction.

A story can have multiple opponents. But it will have only ONE Main Opponent. If you choose to have more than one, the purpose of ALL opponents is to develop different aspects of the hero.

#### Example: Smokey and the Bandit (by Charles Schyer)

<img width="250" height="285" alt="smokey" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/9f661fa9-41ee-472b-80c6-6055ddcdf97c" />

Smokey and the Bandit is an example. This film was competently written by Charles Schyer, a top Hollywood screenwriter. There are three opponents in this story. 

- The Main Opponent, the Sheriff, develops how the hero is a TRICKSTER.
  
- The Secondary Opponent (Sally Field) develops how the hero is a FREE SPIRIT who has never settled down.
  
- The THIRD Opponent (Paul Williams, the capitalist) develops how the hero is a pirate-like OPPORTUNIST.
  
A story can have a WEB of opponents to develop different facets of the hero, but it will only have ONE Main Opponent. The purpose of EVERY opponent in the story is to develop who the hero is. 

Stories are about the HERO, not the opponents. An opponent tries to stop the hero from attaining his or her desire, and develops who the hero is.



6.  Rising Conflict

This is the BODY of your story.  This is the longest of the Nine Steps.  If you’re writing a screenplay or a novel, this is the big empty middle.  

This is “No-Man’s Land.”  

How to develop interest in the Big Empty Middle is every writer’s biggest problem.

In general, what happens in this section is:

The Hero has a desire

The Main Opponent has a counter-desire 

The Hero has a plan

The Main Opponent has a counter-plan 

Both Hero and Opponent become more desperate and ruthless

It’s punch, counter-punch … punch, counter-punch … and this section has to rise in intensity.

Beginnings and endings are relatively EASY to write. The RISING CONFLICT section is where the hard work is. As the conflict rises, your story has to take unexpected turns.

Your plot has to develop in ways the audience doesn’t expect.

(We’ll talk more about how to get MIDDLE INTEREST in a minute.)


7.  BATTLE

The BATTLE is where the Hero and Main Opponent fight it out, once and for all.  

The Battle is the most INTRICATELY written part of your story.  Everything happens fast – and everything has to be significant.

In the BATTLE, the main characters throw their ultimate weapons at each other in rapid succession.  A good battle sequence should have TONS of info in it that you’ve set up, and it should be choreographed like an intricate dance.  


EXAMPLE: It’s a Wonderful Life

George Bailey, who wanted to escape the town, realizes the town needs him.  He realizes only he can save the town from the greedy Potter.

Potter, who has stolen money from the Hero, attempting to financially ruin him, calls in George Bailey’s loan.

George Bailey can’t cover the loan. The he discovers he has the support of the community as people offer small amounts of money to help.  

The townspeople realize they need Bailey.  

Soon, hundreds of people come forward to help cover the debt, which threatens Potter’s plan.

The battle takes on unexpected scope because the whole community comes into play.

With the town’s help, Bailey attains enough money to cover the loan.

Potter realizes he’s lost the battle.  

Bailey’s house, which was a symbol of being trapped in a life he didn’t want, becomes a symbol of his freedom.


ALL this comes into play in the battle — one concept after another, in rapid succession.  It’s intricately composed.  The timing of every event is like a choreographed ballet.  




COURTROOM DRAMA — battle sequence is an intricate ballet of arguments.

WAR STORY — battles are an intricate ballet of attacks and counter attacks.

LOVE STORIES — battle sequence is an intricate interplay of the characters values.  


A Battle becomes exciting when every event has a specific meaning to the HERO and other characters. 

Explosions and car crashes DON’T make a battle exciting. The WORST battle scenes are the ones that have nothing but EXPLOSIONS.

You can USE explosions and car crashes to increase SCOPE, but they DON’T create interest.

The Battle is where:

The hero has his deepest revelations

The opponents have their deepest revelations

The audience understands the deepest thematic revelations



There are FIVE possible outcomes to the Battle:

The Hero WINS and the Opponent LOOSES 
(Raiders of the Lost Ark, It’s a Wonderful Life)


The Opponent WINS and the Hero LOOSES. 
(Easy Rider, Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid)


BOTH the Hero and the Opponent WIN 
(Rocky, A Clockwork Orange)


BOTH the Hero and the Opponent LOSE 
(First Blood, Dr. Zhivago, It’s a Mad Mad World)


The Hero transcends his desire to have the thing he thought he wanted
(A Christmas Carol, Circle of Iron)





8.  SELF REVELATION

This is where the Hero examines his values in the deepest possible way.  

“VALUES” mean his most fundamental belief system.  
Deep values are NOT surface opinions like “the republicans are ruining the country” or “gangs ruined my neighborhood.”  

These are the Hero’s most deeply held beliefs about who he is and how the world works.  

These are the beliefs the Hero has based his entire life on.  They’re so ingrained, up until now he’s hasn’t even been aware of them.

This is the scene where he REALIZES that he HAS these beliefs.

He suddenly realizes how he’s put his entire life and world together. 

This is the “What have I been doing with my life?” moment.  

The Hero REALIZES what his MORAL FLAW is (that you established at the beginning of the story).

He REALIZES his values have caused his UNSATISFACTORY LIFE (that you set up at the beginning of your story).

This is where the hero either TRANSCENDS or is DESTROYED.  

The key to writing this well is it has to be precise and explicit. 


EXAMPLE: Rocky

The Hero’s Self Revelation was the climax of the entire story.  

Rocky gets a lucky punch and almost knocks Apollo out in the first round. 

He realizes he really DOES have a chance at the title.  That he CAN go the distance.  That he’s NOT a victim of circumstances. 

That was the moment Rocky’s whole life changed.  He realized he wasn’t a loser.  That in America even a bum can rise to be a champion. 

Everything that had been set up about Rocky’s life during the entire story changed in those few seconds.  
He REALIZES he’s let the world talk him into believing he’s a loser. 

NOTE: this self-revelation was all done visually and emotionally. 

That’s classy.  In a FIGHT story, Rocky’s self-revelation was clear and specific — and it was also SUBTLE.



EXAMPLE: Oedipus Rex

The story is about how the hero, King Oedipus, refuses to see (understand) that he became king by murdering his father and marrying his mother.  

During the story, a series of people bring him evidence, and he REFUSES to add it all up and “see.” 

Eventually, the evidence is so overwhelming, he HAS to see it.  

He REALIZES he became king because he murdered his father and married his mother.  








9.  NEW EQUILIBRIUM

This is where the Hero does something to show that he has either or CHANGED or NOT CHANGED.

This is an ACTION that shows his new set of beliefs.


EXAMPLE:  Rocky

The Hero fights Apollo with new energy and resolve and goes the distance.  


EXAMPLE:  A Christmas Carol

Scrooge brings a Christmas turkey and presents to Cratchet’s house, and says, “Merry Christmas one and all.” 


EXAMPLE:  Oedipus Rex

The hero can’t handle the terrible knowledge so he STILL refuses to see it (symbolically) by poking his eyes out with with broach pins.  

Since he’s no longer fit to be a king, he goes off to wander the land as a blind beggar.

This is an example of a hero who is DESTROYED by his self revelation.  


The NEW EQUILIBRIUM is a ACTION that shows the Hero’s new concept of morality.  

AND it shows the hero’s new position in the world.

## MIDDLE INTEREST

Let’s talk about the BIG EMPTY MIDDLE part of your story.  

This is “No-Man’s Land.”  

How to develop MIDDLE INTEREST is the biggest problem for every writer.

In general, what happens in this section is:

The Hero has a desire

The Main Opponent has a counter-desire 

The Hero has a plan

The Main Opponent has a counter-plan

The hero and opponent throw punches and counter-punches

Both Hero and Opponent become more desperate and ruthless

But this doesn’t tell you much.

Traditional Act Structure doesn’t help solve the middle interest problem, at all.  

HORACE, a Roman poet 65 BCE), said dramas have a five-act structure.  

	Act 1: Exposition

	Act 2: Rising Action

	Act 3: Climax

	Act 4: Falling Action

	Act 5: Denouement 
	
Gustav Freytag, a German playwright and novelist,who lived during the 1800s, is famous for a visual representation of this called “Freytag’s Pyramid.”

Forget This!  

It’s an old method that doesn’t apply to modern writing.  It’s useless, except as a historical research project.





So is THREE ACT STRUCTURE:

Act 1: Exposition, Inciting Incident, Plot Point One

Act 2: Confrontation, Rising Action, (Turning Point) Plot Point Two 

Act 3: Pre Climax, Climax, Denouement


This comes from Syd Field (1935 - 2012).  It helps a little, but it’s vague and doesn’t address advanced structures, at all.  

It’s NOT that useful.

Because the Middle Section of Rising Conflict is the hardest part of your story to write, INSTEAD of theory about “turning points” and “plot points”.…

Here are THREE PRACTICAL TOOLS you can use to organize your material, generate ideas, and get middle interest.

### THE FOUR Ds

This is a tool you can use over and over, several times in a story.

In the middle of your story, the Hero will have a PLAN, the Opponent will have a COUNTER-PLAN, and so on. 

Whenever the HERO acts on something, there are FOUR STEPS to the process.


DISCOVERY.  

The Hero finds something out about what he has to do to attain his desire.  

He might see an OBSTACLE in his way

He might discover a specific POWER the Opponent has over him  

 He might discover a CLUE that helps him  

He might discover a NEW PATH  

He might discover a special TOOL that helps him 

He might discover a TASK he has to perform


DECISION.  

Once the Hero has made this discovery, he has to DECIDE what to do next.  

The hero has to look within himself and evaluate if his MAIN DESIRE is worth it. 

He thinks about the IMPLICATIONS of making this decision.  

He thinks about the amount of WORK it’s going to take.  

He thinks about WHO COULD GET HURT in the process. 

He thinks about the RESPONSIBILITY he’s about to undertake.


He WEIGHS all this against the VALUE of his MAIN desire. 

Is the thing he desires worth it?  Is it important enough to continue?



DESIGN.  

Once the Hero decides to continue, he has to DESIGN a plan.

Does he have to gather special resources?

Does he have to undergo special training?

Does he have to figure out an intricate procedure?

Does he have to get help from other characters?



DRIVE.  

The Hero then puts his plan into action.  He stops analyzing stuff and just plows forward and ACTS.

This is where you show the SPECIAL POWERS of the Hero.  

Audiences LIKE to see the Hero show off his SKILLS.

The Hero can demonstrate great STRENGTH or ENDURANCE

The Hero can demonstrate FIGHTING SKILLS - maybe he’s a great MARTIAL ARTIST

Or he’s a great INTELLECT (like Sherlock Holmes).

He has to think FAST on his feet as he adapts to unexpected circumstances

Maybe he’s a TRICKSTER (Beverly Hills Cop)

Or a MECHANICAL GENIUS (McGiver)

Or he understands MILITARY TACTICS (Rambo)


DISCOVERY

DECISION

DESIGN

DRIVE


These four steps are like an ENGINE you can use, over and over, as you write your story.  

This is how you DEVELOP the punch-counter-punch between the Hero and the Main Opponent.

I’ve described these from the Hero’s point of view.  But you can use these same steps to develop your Main Opponent, too.  

You can CROSSCUT between you Hero and Opponent using the FOUR D’s against each other.
	
You can also EMPHASIZE or DE-EMPHASIZE any one of these steps.  

Emphasize DECISION — make it hard for the hero to decide to go on.

Emphasize PLAN — some genres (WAR STORIES) are all-out emphasizing PLAN.


You can use the FOUR D’s to DEVELOP MATERIAL.  If you don’t have an idea, think about emphasizing one or more of the FOUR Ds.

You can use the FOUR D’s to AVOID CLICHES.


EXAMPLE:  Take this cliche — the Hero loses the girl so he walks alone in the rain.  

Try emphasizing DECISION instead.  It’s a hard decision to go into the rain.  But he realizes he has no choice.


They tell you to avoid cliches. But writing a cliche is not bad — it’s a kind of SHORTHAND.  You can “un-cliche” the idea in a later draft, and one of the ways to do it is to emphasize a different one of the “Four Ds.” 

You can use the FOUR Ds to GET VARIETY.  

You don’t want to write drive … drive … drive … and nothing else. 

You can change your pacing by extending a decision, or by creating a intricate plan…

### THINK IN SEQUENCES

The Hero’s Main Desire can’t be too easy to get or there’s no story. 

Achieving the Main Desire has to be complex, and when you’re confronted with a difficult process, you break it up into a series a smaller goals.  

A SEQUENCE is a group of scenes that gets the hero from Point A to Point B.  



EXAMPLE: Sally Meets John

You could write, “At the party Sally sees John.”

That’s not interesting.  

To make it interesting, turn it into a SEQUENCE.  For Example,

1. Sally discovers her mother has just married a guy named “John,” so she goes to meet the new husband.  

2. She drives to her childhood cape cod house but nobody answers the door.

3. She finds the hidden key, goes in, and takes her luggage upstairs.

4. She hears the shower running, goes in the bedroom and sees a suitcase with the name “John.”  He’s in the shower singing “Good Vibrations” loudly and enjoying the echo. 

5. The singing stops, so she leaves the bedroom — to avoid being caught snooping.  

6. She realizes she forgot her phone.  She turns back to get it, and sees a young handsome man standing there looking at her.  He doesn’t even bother covering cup with the towel.  

6. He says, “Who are you?”

She says, “I live here.  Who are you?”

“John Smith.”

“You’re my mother’s new husband?  How dare you take advantage of her.  Are you a gigolo?  What are you after — her money?”

He says, “No, I’m John Smith Junior.”


Now you have what every writer needs: 4 scenes.




You build a modern story out of sequences, NOT acts.  

In spite of all you hear about “Act Structure,” nobody really knows what an “ACT” is.  (It’s never been defined.)

But you KNOW what a SEQUENCE is.

A SEQUENCE is a group of SCENES — that gets the hero from point A to point B. 


In a novel, sequences tend to be be CHAPTERS. 

Screenwriters and TV writers tend to think in terms of groups of SCENES. 

Thinking in sequences helps you create material for the Big Empty Middle, which is your biggest story problem.

Don’t just think in terms of SCENES, think in SEQUENCES.

THEN turn your sequences into groups of scenes.

### BENDING THE DESIRE LINE

This is different from BUILDING the desire line, which we talked about earlier (going from a less powerful desire to a more powerful one). 

BENDING the desire line is based on this Important Principle:


SOMETHING GOES WRONG!


It works like this:


The Hero tells what his DESIRE is (for a specific sequence).

This sets up an EXPECTATION in the mind of the audience.  When the Hero says he wants to do something, the audience has a NATURAL EMPATHY for him — we want him to succeed.  

The Hero DOES attain his desire – but NOT in the way he or the audience expected.  


You tell the audience (reader) what to anticipate.  You give them what they expect —  but not in the way they expected it.

Audiences LOVE to say, “I knew that was going to happen but I didn’t think it would happen that way.”  If you get that reaction, you’re a success.

You can use this to make your sequences more interesting.  For example,



EXAMPLE: “Getting the Job”

In this SEQUENCE, SALLY, wants to get the job.

She puts on a new dress.  She takes extra effort to make sure she looks great.

She goes outside but can’t get a cab.  She’ll have to walk across the park to get to the office.

She hears thunder.  It starts to rain.  

She ducks under a bridge.  Unfortunately, there’s a gang, there, from El Salvador with skull tattoos on their faces.

She has no choice but to dash into the rain and run to the curb where a car drives by and splashes her with mud.

When she gets to her location, she sees her reflection in a window.  She’s a mess, she decides to give up on the interview.  

As she turns to go, she bumps into someone else who is also soaked head to foot — and discovers it’s her new boss.


The audience GOT what they were told to expect — she got the interview and the job.  But not in the way the audience expected.  

This is all based on: SOMETHING GOES WRONG.





ANOTHER way you can do this is put LITTLE  conflicts in between BIG conflicts.

CONFLICT is what drives your story forward, but you can’t have big conflicts all the time.  So you put LITTLE conflicts in between the big ones.


EXAMPLE:

The Hero goes in the bartender and asks, “Do you know where I can get some cigarettes?” 

Bartender says, “Do I look like I a cigarette machine?” 

The hero then sees a news stand with cigs just outside, and gets them.


This doesn’t mean anything to the overall story.  It’s just a little conflict along the way.  

It’s a little touch that gives your story forward momentum.  


Conclusion:

These techniques can help GENERATE and ORGANIZE your middle material.

The Four “Ds” — “engine” of the story

Sequencing

Bending the Desire Line using SOMETHING GOES WRONG.

Put little Conflicts Between Big Conflicts

## THE TWO STORY FORMS

In very general terms, there are two different kinds of story. 

The HEROIC JOURNEY and the DRAMA.  

These are two fundamentally different strategies of story telling.    

The most ancient story form is the Heroic Journey.  The Drama developed second.  

## THE DRAMA

The drama uses VERTICAL structure.  

Vertical writing is when the Hero stays in the same place, locked together with the other characters.  

In a house 
(Cat on a Hot Tin Roof, The Glass Menagerie).

In the city’s business world 
(Working Girl, Emily in Paris)

In the low rent side of town.
(Rocky)

Hogwarts School for Wizards
Morpeheus’ conception of the Matrix

Dramatic (vertical) writing develops:

The KINDS of POWER one character holds over another

Who holds power over whom

How the power SHIFTS from one character to another

How one character increases his tactics of oppression over another


Dramas usually develop a SMALL number of characters to a LARGE degree.  

When I gave you the NINE TRADITIONAL STEPS, I was talking mostly about how they are used in DRAMAS, which is the most common kind of story. Heroic Journeys are more rare.  

## THE HEROIC JOURNEY

The other story form is the Heroic Journey.  It uses HORIZONTAL structure.  

HORIZONTAL writing is when the Hero goes on a journey, from one place, to the next, and the next, and so on.
In journey stories — like in The Wizard of Oz —  the Hero encounters exotic places, strange characters she’s never met before, extraordinary opponents, and difficult trials. 

The Heroic Journey usually develops a LARGE number of characters to a SMALL degree. 

Examples:

	The Incredible Journey (about the Lobrador retreiver)
	Gulliver’s Travels (not a pure journey from story)
	Candide


The DRAMA and the HEROIC JOURNEY are the TWO STORY FORMS.  

They are the two fundamental strategies you can use to write a story.  

The biggest differences between them are:

Vertical vs. Horizontal structure

The kind of story world

The kind of character development

The kind of theme

The heroic Journey developed BEFORE the drama.  The most ancient stories are heroic journeys.  



Gilgamesh
The Odyssey
Jason and the Argonauts
Moses (The Ten Commandments)



The Hero goes on a journey, not to see strange new things, but to learn what is within himself.  

These are ALWAYS circular stories.  The Hero ends up (in some sense) in the place where he began.  

He learns what was always within himself but didn’t know.  He has to go on the journey to find it out.

Here are the 8 special steps of the Heroic Journey.  

These were compiled by Joseph Campbell and they appear in mythologies all over the world.

Remember, these are ANCIENT stories — so as we go through these steps, you can see how they anticipated the later DRAMA form:

1.
The Hero’s Birth

The Hero has a DIFFICULT birth or a MYSTICAL birth.

A DIFFICULT birth means it’s a difficult world.  It’s a struggle just to exist.  It shows the difficulty of the human condition.  The hardship of the human condition.  

A MYSTICAL birth shows there’s a relationship between Heaven and Earth.  Between the supernatural and the real.  Between the Gods and Man.  


2.
Loss of the Father

The hero looses his father, or his community, or a close friend.  

This makes another statement about the human condition:  man is alone.  

The Hero has to find himself without help.  


3.
The Call to Adventure

A mysterious event happens.  

The Hero is LURED or CARRIED away from his normal, commonplace world.

This is an early version of the INCITING INCIDENT. 

In this sequence, the hero goes from complacence to a world of adventure.  From a normal world to a supernatural world.  

And this leads him to having a DESIRE.  

The Hero learns his DESIRE is his DESTINY.

Sometimes there is a supernatural character (a wizard or a prophet) who introduces the Hero to his destiny. 

The Hero’s Desire is also a PUBLIC DESIRE.  He needs to achieve it for himself — but also for the benefit of a community.


4.
The Gateway

This is a supernatural threshold.  It’s a passage between worlds.  

In the Wizard of Oz, it’s being carried away by the tornado.

In Alice in Wonderland, it’s falling down the rabbit hole.

Sometimes there’s a GUARDIAN of the Gateway, and the Hero has to defeat the Guardian. 

(Sometimes the Hero is killed by the Guardian and then descends into the Underworld and has to struggle to get back to his world again.)
5.
The Journey

During the journey, the Hero encounters several OPPONENTS.  These can be super-men, supernatural beings, forces of nature, or Gods.  

The hero meets supernatural things because we define what “human” is by comparing it to the supernatural.  


On the journey, the Hero may find an ALLY.  These are characters that help the Hero.  In the Wizard of Oz, this is the Scarecrow, the Tin Man, the Cowardly Lion.  

On the journey, the Hero passes through TRIALS – these are tests of human skills.  They test his strength.  Test his intelligence.  Test his endurance.  These trials put human qualities to the test.

The Hero may find a TALISMAN – this is a significant object that has a special power that helps the Hero attain his desire. 


The JOURNEY is the older version of the big, empty middle in the drama.

WARNING: because horizontal writing is so episodic, it’s difficult to build RISING conflict.  This is an inherent weakness of the journey form.

This is why you never see the heroic journey in its pure form written anymore.


6.
Visit to Death
	
The Hero sees his own death.  Confronts his own mortality.

He sees the ULTIMATE LAW that controls all humanity.

Sometimes, this includes a visit to the Underworld.

In the Christ Story, this was the line, “Father, why have you forsaken me?”


7.
The Hero’s Self Revelation

In the Heroic Journey form, the Hero has a specific KIND of self revelation.

FIRST, he proves the best way to BE HUMAN:

Maybe physical strength is the best human quality (Hercules)

Maybe glory is the best human achievement (Jason and the Argonauts, The Iliad)

Maybe the best human achievement is to be King and lead a community (The Thief of Baghdad)


SECOND, the Hero’s SELF REVELATION includes a community.  His revelation has meaning for him — and it has meaning for ALL people.

It’s both a PRIVATE and a PUBLIC revelation.  
	
He discovers a new way of living for all people. (Moses story) (The Ten Commandments).

In the Christ Story, this was the the line “Forgive them, they know not what they do.”


8.
The Hero is Profoundly Changed

Often, the Hero becomes fabulously rich.  

The community that he lost at the beginning of the story, he now leads.

There’s often a opulent marriage.

Sometimes he even undergoes APOTHEOSIS – the Hero is elevated to being a deity (Christ Story).


## THEME

Theme is an argument that you, the writer, make. You put it in your story. 
	
It can be a MORAL argument or a HUMAN argument.
	
It’s the author’s statement about how people should behave within the world of your story. Then your story makes a comment on reality.

### HOW TO DEVELOP THEME

To write a theme, you develop an argument between a THEME vs. a COUNTER-THEME. It’s an argument, and a counter argument.

For example, in a detective story,
	The criminal believes, “Crime pays.” 
	The detective investigates and believes, “Crime shouldn’t pay.” 
	The criminal thinks, “He’s just a dumb cop; crime pays.” 			The detective closes in; he’ll make sure crime doesn’t pay. 
	And so on…

Idea vs. idea is how you develop any argument. 

Theme is NOT just a statement you make that shows up at the end of your story. Just like you build a character over the arc of your story, you also build your THEMATIC ARGUMENTS and THEMATIC COMMENTS over the course of your story.

As your story plays out, your theme develops.

Theme has a line of development: Exposition, example, revelation point, side comments, powerful appeals to emotion, a closing statement. 
	These are all tools of the essay, of rhetoric.

If you have a powerful thematic revelation for the end of your story, you’re HOT. 

### LIGHT TOUCH

Theme is a light touch. Don’t make every scene or every conflict about your theme. Less is more. 

Put the pieces of your thematic argument, here and there, lightly through your story. 

Don’t just put it in dialogue, you can put it in your hero’s actions, how you juxtapose scenes, through the kinds of conflict your hero encounters.

Putting theme only in dialogue (or putting in too many thematic scenes) makes it heavy-handed. No subtlety. Makes your story strained (Woody Allen).

Heavy handed theme is: Days of Wine and Roses or Guess Who’s Coming to Dinner. Those are morality plays.

### THE THEME SCALE

Audiences don’t like it if they’re hit over the head with your theme. They want a story that makes them think, they don’t want to be told what to think. 

There’s a scale of obviousness in theme building. 

At one end of the THEME SCALE, you have clearly conclusive. Morality plays are clearly conclusive: characters learn (or don’t learn) clear moral lessons. 
	Dragnet. 
	Detective stories and Police Proceedurals are morality plays. 
	Melodrama. 
	
At the other end of the scale is the ambiguous conclusion. At the end of the story, you don’t know what conclusion you should draw.
	A Clockwork Orange.
	Was the state right or wrong when they took away Alex’s free will?
	Was Alex redeemed at the end? Nobody knows. But it makes you think. 
	[Blade Runner, 5 Easy Pieces, 2001, The Graduate]


The two poles of the Theme Scale: clearly conclusive or completely ambiguous.


Trans:
	
Not all themes are moral arguments.
	Lawrence of Arabia, Rocky, Circle of Iron, Dr. Zhivago
These stories seem to make bigger statements than just taking a side over some moral issue. 
	
Somehow, they simultaneously make a larger statement about people’s place in the WORLD, or in the COSMOS.

That’s because those thematic argument are about what it means to be HUMAN.

There are two kinds of thematic statements: 
		What it means to be moral
		What it means to be human
	
These are the two big issues that stories are built to talk about. It’s why there are two major dramatic forms: the heroic journey and the drama. 

Sophisticated stories mix these up. They make combo statements. 

They develop “what is morality?” and a “What’s the best way to be human?” simultaneously.

	Lawrence of Arabia. 
	As Lawrence puts the tribes together to form a nation, he’s putting himself together to form a whole man. 
	Has he done the tribes any good? The morality is of what he’s done is ambiguous.
	He got to experience being a powerful leader. Did he make himself a better man? Is the use of power the best way to be human?
	Dr Zhivago (the film) Can love conquer all when you live in a tyranny? [the power of love is human, plus ambiguous answer]

    ## PHILOSOPHICAL STATEMENTS

    The Heroic Journey developed to talk about the HUMAN CONDITION.  

The HEROIC JOURNEY is the earliest form of story — and it was MADE to talk about what it means to be human, the FIRST great question of humanity

The DRAMA developed later.  It was made to talk about the SECOND great question of humanity — “What does it mean to be MORAL?”

To this day, we’re STILL asking these two fundamental questions.

ESPECIALLY today — with SURVEILLANCE, ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE, and BRAIN IMPLANTS rise, we’re asking “How do we deal with this MORALLY?” and “What does it mean to be HUMAN?”

A thematic story has to say something interesting about one or both of these questions.  Which means, a writer also has to be a PHILOSOPHER.  So, here’s two lists of philosophical viewpoints on these two questions to get you started. 

These lists gives you the RANGE of philosophical statements you can make about the big questions, 

“What does it mean to be HUMAN?”

“What does it mean to be MORAL?”

You can use these to build themes. You can also use these to build character viewpoints.

Let’s start with “What does it mean to be HUMAN?”  The FIRST and simplest philosophical statement is…

1.
Animalism / Hedonism

In ANIMALISM, people are motivated by sensual and physical appetites only.  Not moral, spiritual or intellectual.

There is no humanity.  Man is just an animal.  His big brain is just a tool for satisfying animal instincts.

Example: The Naked Prey

HEDONISM (which is right next to Animalism) says that pursuit of pleasure is the only valid goal of human life.

Example: Juliette by Marquis de Sade

Both Animalism and Hedonism say that SELF INTEREST is the only valid reason for action.  


2.
Omnipotence

Man is a conqueror.  Nature bends to man’s will.  Technology is good because it brings nature under man’s control. 

A Disney documentary, c 1955:  “Forests are conquered.  Trees fall like toothpicks as America builds its mighty freeways.”

Omnipotence says, humanity’s ultimate achievement are glory and triumph.

Examples: 

Patton  
The Illiad
Leni Riefenstahl’s Triumph of the Will.  


3.
Existentialism / Absurdism

This Point of View says, Man really has no purpose, at all.

He is absurd because he tries to be rational in an irrational universe. 

All he can do is make choices between things that happen to him.  He’s stuck trying to make the best out of the way things are. 

He frets and sweats, but ultimately, he is RIDICULOUS. Sartre: the nunivers has no meaning so humans have to create their own meaning even though it’s absurd.

Man can either know he’s insignificant or NOT know he’s insignificant.  It doesn’t matter.

Theater of the Absurd is an entire genre dedicated to this Point of View:

Examples: 

Beckett’s Waiting for Godot.  

Tom Stoppard’s Rosencrans and Guildenstern Are Dead.  

Eugene Ionesco’s The Bald Soprano.


4.
Buddhism

Life is suffering.  Because man has constant desire, he is never satisfied.  

Man suffers because he’s a slave to lust, envy, and wanting to possess things.

The carrot is always just out of reach, and he always has to settle for less than he deserves. 

An old TV show called, The Rockford Files.

(James Garner). This was the key to the audience bond with his character.  He was a sincere fighter for justice in a city world, but was never rewarded for the good work he did. 



5.
The Achiever

Man is a goal accomplisher.  He builds to achieve higher and higher goals.  

Stories about the scientist, the artist – also, the fascist.   

Man’s rationality and goal achievement is a reflection of a higher order. 

Man is worthy and dignified and created in God’s image.  The universe is super-rational.

Examples: 

Biography of Thomas Edison.  

Life of Louise Pasteur.  

Atlas Shrugged.  

These are stories that glorify individualism.  

The ROMANTIC MOVEMENT (Shelley, Byron, Blake…) was all about individualism (as a reaction to logical positivism and industrialization). 



6.
Quixotism

Man’s highest purpose is drama and adventure.  

To confront all the wrongs in the world head-on, no matter what the consequences.

Example: 

	Don Quixote.  

There’s an entire genre dedicated to this POV called “The Traveling Angel” story.  (Will talk about that in the class on Character.)

Another version of this says, man’s highest nature is to be playful.  

Example: 

Close Encounters.  


7.
Transcendentalism

Man is spiritual in nature.  

In seeking his own purpose, he’s a seeker of the purpose of the universe. 

Transcendentalism says that universal law permeates everyone and every thing.  To find the entire universe, you only needs to look inside yourself.  

and the TRANSCENDENTALISTS (Thoreau, Emerson, Walt Whitman…) were all about this.

Example:

Circle of Iron.  (a martial arts movie)

## MORAL SYSTEMS

The Heroic Journey is full of statements about what it means to be HUMAN.  

DRAMA FORM, on the other hand, is built to talk about, “What does it mean to be MORAL?”  

In stories, MORALITY only pertains to how humans act towards each other.  

There is no such thing as a moral or immoral thought.  There are only moral or immoral ACTIONS towards other characters.  

IMPORTANT NOTE: There is no such thing as a moral or immoral act towards SOCIETY.  “Society” is an abstraction.  A character can be accused of “acting against society” — but it’s really ALWAYS one individual acting immorally against another individual. 

This is why, when a hero leads a community (or a tyrant opresses a community) you have to show how it’s an INDIVIDUAL effecting other INDIVIDUALS.  “Society” is an abstraction based on a series of individual circumstances.

Moral arguments ask, “What actions are RIGHT?” and “What actions are WRONG?”

Here’s the RANGE of philosophical statements it’s possible to make about what it means to be MORAL.

1.
“There Is No Morality.”

This says, there is no such thing as right or wrong.  Only MIGHT makes RIGHT.  

If you want something, take it.  What happens to other people is irrelevant.  The important thing is to WIN. People admire and follow you because you’re a winner.  HOW you win doesn’t matter.

Example: 

Film (1971): A Clockwork Orange 
	   Film Lord of the Rings?
Film (1978): Straight Time.  


2.
“Absolute Right.”

This says that some things are RIGHT and some things are WRONG. 

If you do wrong, you must be punished.  

Things are black or white.  “You broke the law, you must be BROUGHT TO JUSTICE.”  

Justice is punishment.

Examples: 

Detective Stories 
Police Stories  
Westerns
Political Dramas


3.
“Relative Right”
	
This says RIGHT and WRONG do exist but they vary depending on context.  

Justice depends on circumstances.  Called “Situational Ethics.”

The ultimate standard is whatever promotes justice for the HERO — in THIS individual situation.  

This POV says, “Although we don’t know what justice means for society at large, we can see that it is done in this case.”

Examples: 

Robin Hood.  
Courtroom Dramas. 
Investigative Journalism stories:
	Absence of Malice
	The Verdict 


AND there’s another way to use this concept as a theme:

In some stories, the individual gets screwed by the system.  These stories say the system SHOULD HAVE promoted justice for the individual.  
Example: 

Film (1973): Serpico.
Film (1974): Chinatown.


4.  
“Complete Morality”

This system says morality can be achieved in one case and for ALL society, simultaneously.  

The Hero CAN see the ultimate effects of his moral decision.
	
Examples: 

It’s a Wonderful Life.  
Gandhi. 
Atlas Shrugged.

You have to be a top writer and philosopher to pull this kind of story off.

In the future, important Big-Vision epics are NOT going to be religious stories. They’re going to stories about the successes and failures of COMPLETE MORALITY.  

Today, most stories about the future depict ULTIMATE SLAVERY.  The dystopian future.  That’s a tired-out idea now.  

The next great epic won’t be about digital world tyranny, it will be about digital world FREEDOM.

## CONCLUSION - FINAL THOUGHTS ON PLOT CONSTRUCTION

The structure of stories is based on what happens in life.  

ALL problems, big or small, have these characteristics. 

These are the steps any person would take to solve a problem.  

If you set out to do something significant in your life, STORY STRUCTURE describes what can happen to you – from surface patterns to encountering the deepest questions.

This is why stories are popular.  

It’s why they’ve been the most popular art form for thousands of years.  

People relate the structure of stories to the structure of their own lives.
