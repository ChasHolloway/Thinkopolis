Henry David Thoreau was the son of a businessman. 

His father made pencils, and because of a graphite discovery in NH, in 1821, they were the finest pencils in America. 

Growing up, young Henry David loved Greek and Roman classics, he loved natural history and math.

He went to Harvard, said he got little benefit from it, but he liked the library.

When he was twenty, he met Ralph Waldo Emerson, they became fast friends, and together they created the New England Transcendalist Movement. It would become one of the most significant literary developments of the 1800s.

Transcendentalism celebrated the individual. It promoted emotion over reason, nature over man.  It elevated intuition.

In 1840, Emerson started a transcendentalist magazine called “The Dial,” and published Thoreau’s essays. 

Issues sold only two to three hundred copies. 
	
Henry David searched and searched for a teaching job, but was continually rejected. “My essays are bait that will not tempt the rats,” he said.

He had to work at the family pencil factory. 
Soon, he earned enough to pick a spot on some land Emerson owned and with help of friends, build a small cabin. 

On the 4th of July, 1845, when he was 28-years-old, he moved in.
	
It was his most productive period. He wrote essays about living off the land, growing his own food, and communing with nature. 
	
The truth was, though, his cabin was a quarter mile from a village where he dined at the local pubs every night.
	
He wasn’t living off nature any more than Bruce Springstein is a working man.
	
He also wrote that he would not support a government that endorsed slavery, and refused to pay taxes. His essays were published, and he was arrested. 

Thoreau’s response was to give lectures on what he called, “Civil Disobedience.”
	
He claimed there was a higher law than a civil one.
	
Eventually, Thoreau’s passion for transcendentalism drained away, and he took over the family’s businesss. 

He remained anti-slavery, and even helped bring former slaves to freedom through the Underground Railroad. 
	
When he learned that the fiery abolitionist John Brown had been  hanged for starting a slave revolt, people said he lost the will to live, himself. 
	
	
When he died at 44-years old, people said he was model for how NOT to life one’s life. 
	
Publishers dumped unsold copies of his books on his porch. 
	
Everyone, including Thoreau, himself, thought he was a failure. 
	
In a hundred years, that would all change.
	
Obscure until the mid-20th century, his books became hugely popular during the beat and hippie movements.  

And he’s been popular ever since.
