## The Script

Hypatia
by Chas Holloway
(DRAFT: 4-12-25)
381 words

	
The year was 370 C.E. when Hypatia, daughter of Theon, a mathematician and astronomer, was born.

She grew up running through the halls and rooms of her father’s museum, a part of the Great Library of Alexandria. Under his care, by the time she was a teen, she had become the greatest living mathematician in the world. 

The classical world then, like America today, was in decline. Hypatia, however, was a Neo-Platonist, fighting against the fall.   

Neo-Platonism was a philosophical movement started by Plotinus a hundred years before. It was to be a grand synthesis of all knowledge that had been created over the preceeding thousand years. It was also to be the last development of the ancient world.

Afterwards, Christianity would take over emphasizing heaven and hell, and for the next thousand years, moral philosophy would dominate the western world. Natural philosophy would be almost forgotten.

While Christian sects warred throughout the city of Alexandria, Hypatia became famous for rising above it. 
	
News of her scientific skills spread throughout the ancient world. People heard she’d built an instrument called an astrolabe to measure the position of the stars. She’s built another device to measure the density of liquids. They heard she’d written and edited a 13 volume compendium on algebra, a new and mysterious subject at the time. She was as beautiful as Athena, people said, and she surpassed all the best-known men of her time in philosophy. 

People came from far and wide — as far away as India — to study under her. 

Her huge popularity angered a man named Cyril, a Bishop, who was the top Christian official in Alexandria at the time. He saw the Great Library as an evil furnace of paganism and sorcery, and Hypatia was its hottest flame. 
	
So, in the year 415, at 35 years old, while she was riding a chariot to the library, a Christian mob blocked her way and dragged her out. They tore off her clothes, dragged her to a nearby church and sliced her to death with sharpened oyster shells.

Her murder marked the death of the classical world and the beginning of the dark ages. The entire Library of Alexandria was burned to the ground not long after. 

You’re listening to Thinkopolis. I’m Chas Holloway.
